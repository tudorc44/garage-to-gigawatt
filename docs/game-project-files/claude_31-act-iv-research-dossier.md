## Orbital Compute Sells Time, Not Cheap Power

*Research dossier for doc 31 of Garage to Gigawatt (Act IV, 2031-2035): AI data centres in Earth orbit, supplied and eventually built from the Moon. Evidence current to 5 October 2026, synthesised from research notes 01-09 and the design thread's own cost model (note 10). Evidence tiers used throughout: [A] demonstrated or contracted, [B] announced and funded plans, [C] credible expert projection, [D] speculation or hype.*

## 1. What the evidence actually supports

As of 5 October 2026, putting AI data centres in orbit is a real, richly funded race that is still at the demonstration stage. What actually flies is tiny: one NVIDIA H100 graphics processor (GPU, the chip that runs AI) on Starcloud's 60 kg Starcloud-1 since November 2025, Google's first Suncatcher prototype with Google's own AI chips since 1 October 2026, and China's 12-satellite Three-Body constellation running small AI models. Nobody has flown even one 100-kilowatt compute satellite, let alone the gigawatt-scale "data centres in space" that SpaceX, Starcloud and Chinese state plans describe. The money is enormous and real: SpaceX absorbed xAI and raised $75 billion in the largest IPO in history, Starcloud is valued at $2.3 billion, and regulatory filings cover up to 1 million compute satellites. Independent cost studies put orbit at roughly 2-4.4 times the cost of the same AI compute on the ground today; the best credible projections, and the design thread's own model, narrow that to about 1.2-1.6 times by 2031-2035, with parity only if cheap launch, light satellites, cheap satellite manufacturing and expensive or blocked ground power all arrive together. Orbit's real edge in the game's window is speed, not price: a block of satellites can be built and launched in about four to six quarters (design-thread estimate), while new US ground power waits more than five years for a grid connection and faces local opposition that now polls at 75%. The Moon cannot supply orbital data centres at any meaningful scale before 2035.

**What is demonstrated.** Only small things. Starcloud-1 trained a small language model and ran Google's Gemma model in orbit [A]; in Google's ground radiation test its AI chip's memory glitched only at almost three times the five-year dose expected in orbit, and the chip had no hard failure at twenty times it [A, lab]. On-board processing of satellite data ("edge computing") has run in space since 2017 and is the only proven business case [A]. SpaceX's Starship, the fully reusable heavy rocket the sector depends on, first reached orbit with working satellites on 28 September 2026 but has not yet been caught, reused or refuelled in orbit [A]; the only third-party Starship price on record is $90 million for one 2029 launch, about $600-900 per kg [A]. In 2025 SpaceX flew about half the world's launches and roughly 85-90% of the mass, and it is also the most aggressive would-be orbital-compute operator, so everyone else buys lift from their main rival.

**What is hype.**

| Claim (who, when) | Tier | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|
| Space will be "the lowest cost" place for AI compute within 2-3 years (Musk, Jan 2026) | D | No independent model finds orbit cheaper before about 2035; SpaceX's own IPO prospectus says orbital AI compute "may not achieve commercial viability". |
| "100 GW" of compute launched per year (SpaceX FCC filing, Jan 2026) | D | Needs about 1 million tonnes a year in orbit, roughly 370 times everything the world launched in 2025. |
| A 5 GW station, orbit 10-20 times cheaper, "nearly all new data centers" in space within 10 years (Starcloud, 2024-2025) | D | Starcloud has flown one GPU; its cost claim omits the satellites and GPUs and assumes launch at ~$30/kg, a hundredth of today's price. |
| Filings for 1,000,000 (SpaceX), 100,000 (Orbital), 88,000 (Starcloud), 51,600 (Blue Origin) and up to 200,000 (China) satellites | A as filings, D as plans | Claims on orbital shells and spectrum; the rules allow years before anything must be built. |
| Comparable to ground at under $200/kg launch by the mid-2030s (Google, Nov 2025) | C | The most grounded claim, but it compares launch cost with ground electricity only, not the satellite's full cost. |
| The first "data center on the Moon" (Lonestar, 2025) | A that it landed; claim inflated | A 1 kg backup drive on a lander that tipped over and died about 13 hours after landing. |

**What decides the outcome.** Four numbers: launch price per kg, tonnes of satellite per megawatt, the satellite's build cost, and how long GPUs last in orbit. Below a few hundred dollars per kg of launch, the last three matter more than launch, and the GPU bill (about three-quarters of the cost on either side) dominates. On the ground the binding problem is time: grid waits of five-plus years, capacity auctions stuck at their price cap, and about $200 billion of US projects blocked or delayed in the first half of 2026.

### Lunar mining: real physics, unproven resource, no supply before 2035

Water ice at the lunar poles exists, but nobody knows how much, how deep or how concentrated it is at mining scale; the only direct measurement is one 2009 impact plume that found 5.6% water by mass, give or take 2.9 points [A]. Every check on the ground since has failed or slipped: NASA's ice drill on the IM-2 lander (March 2025) measured only rocket exhaust because the lander fell over, NASA's VIPER ice rover is unlikely to land before about 2028-2029, and China's Chang'e-7 prospector was postponed in August 2026 [A]. Nothing has ever been extracted on the Moon; the best ground test made 0.07 kg of oxygen an hour from 25 kg of simulated lunar soil [A, ground]. The reference commercial propellant plant is a 2019 study costing about $4 billion and needing 2.8 MW [C], while NASA's lunar power target is a single 100 kW reactor around 2030 [B]. Commercial landers have fully succeeded once in seven missions since 2019 [A].

The physics advantage is genuine: getting from the lunar surface to a parking point between Earth and Moon takes about a quarter of the speed change needed just to reach low orbit from Earth [A]. But it pays off most for customers in high orbit and around the Moon, and least for data centres in low Earth orbit. In the design model, lunar-made radiators, structure and shielding cut the cost of a low-orbit data centre by only about 0-4% at plausible 2035 volumes (about 10% in an aggressive case) and never touch the GPU bill. Helium-3 is a kilogram-scale market for quantum-computer refrigerators, not a fusion fuel [D for fusion]. Credible 2031-2035 lunar output is pilot plants making 1-100 tonnes a year of oxygen or water for local use; hundreds of tonnes a year shipped to Earth orbit is speculation [D].

## 2. The dossier

### How to read this dossier

Each claim carries its evidence tier and a citation giving the source and its publication date. [A] means demonstrated or contracted: flown hardware, measured data, signed contracts, regulatory filings, closed funding rounds or public financials. [B] means an announced and funded plan. [C] means a credible expert projection. [D] means speculation or hype. "Inference" marks reasoning from cited figures by the researchers (with the note number) or by this dossier, never a source claim. "Not verified" marks something a researcher could not confirm. "Secondary" marks an aggregator or blog summarising a primary source that could not be read. Where sources disagree, both are given and the conflict is named. The recurring jargon is defined below; other terms are defined where they first appear.

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| LEO (low Earth orbit) | Roughly 160-2,000 km up; nearly all orbital data-centre plans sit at 400-1,000 km. |
| Dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit (SSO) | A near-polar orbit that rides the boundary between day and night, so solar panels see the Sun almost all the time. |
| GEO (geostationary orbit) | 35,786 km up, where a satellite appears fixed over one spot; farther, harsher radiation, longer signal delay. |
| Earth-Moon L1 (EML1) | A balance point between Earth and Moon where a spacecraft can hover with little fuel. |
| Delta-v | The change in speed a spacecraft must make; it sets how much propellant a trip needs. |
| ISRU (in-situ resource utilisation) | Making useful products (water, oxygen, metals) from local material instead of shipping them. |
| Regolith | Lunar soil. |
| PSR (permanently shadowed region) | Crater floors near the lunar poles that never see sunlight; the likeliest home of ice. |
| TRL (technology readiness level) | A 1-9 scale from idea (1) to proven in operation (9). |
| IT load, "per MW" | The power drawn by the computers themselves; "per MW" means per megawatt of computing load. |
| PUE (power usage effectiveness) | Total facility power divided by computing power; 1.15 means 15% overhead for cooling and losses. |
| Capex / opex | Upfront capital spending / yearly running costs. |
| Cost of capital (WACC) | The yearly return investors and lenders demand; riskier projects pay more. |
| Levelised cost | Upfront cost spread into equal yearly payments over an asset's life, plus running costs, so options with different lifetimes can be compared. |
| Take-or-pay | A contract in which the customer pays for capacity whether or not it uses it; the basis on which lenders lend. |

### 2.1 Who is pushing orbital compute, and what they have really done

The field has three kinds of backer: companies that own rockets and want to fill them (SpaceX, Blue Origin), chip and cloud companies that sell the hardware or want the capacity (NVIDIA, Google), and venture-funded startups. The test that separates them is flown hardware, and by that test the field is small: "only dozens of advanced GPUs" were in orbit in March 2026, against about 4 million NVIDIA GPUs sold on the ground in 2025 ([TechCrunch, 30 Mar 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/30/starcloud-raises-170-million-series-ato-build-data-centers-in-space)).

#### Starcloud: the only startup with a data-centre GPU in orbit, and the funding leader

Starcloud was founded in January 2024 as Lumen Orbit by Philip Johnston (ex-McKinsey), Ezra Feilden (ex-Airbus) and Adi Oltean (ex-SpaceX and Microsoft Azure), went through Y Combinator and renamed itself in March 2025 after a challenge from Lumen Technologies [A] ([Wikipedia: Starcloud, accessed 5 Oct 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starcloud)). Wikipedia places it in El Segundo, California; GeekWire puts it in Redmond, Washington, with a 100,000 sq ft factory in Woodinville building Starcloud-3, which is the likelier current picture ([GeekWire, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/starcloud-250m-data-center-satellite-network-nvidia/)). **Starcloud-1, about 60 kg with one NVIDIA H100, launched on a SpaceX Falcon 9 in November 2025** [A] ([NVIDIA blog, 15 Oct 2025](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/starcloud/)); an aggregator dates the launch to 2 November ([Introl, 21 Feb 2026](https://introl.com/blog/orbital-data-centers-space-computing-race-2026)). In orbit it trained Andrej Karpathy's small nanoGPT model on the works of Shakespeare (the first language-model training in space), ran inference on Google's open Gemma model and analysed radar imagery for Capella Space [A] ([GeekWire, 22 Dec 2025](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/starcloud-power-training-ai-space/)). Correction carried from note 01: Wikipedia, citing CNBC, says it ran "Gemini"; the primary reporting says Gemma. Starcloud has published no radiation or error-rate data from the H100 (not verified as of 5 October 2026).

Funding has outrun hardware. After about $34 million raised by December 2025, Starcloud closed **$170 million at a $1.1 billion valuation on 30 March 2026** (led by Benchmark and EQT Ventures) and **$250 million at a $2.3 billion post-money valuation on 21 August 2026** (led by Manhattan West, with NVIDIA, reportedly about $25 million, and Cisco), about $450 million in all [A] ([TechCrunch, 30 Mar 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/30/starcloud-raises-170-million-series-ato-build-data-centers-in-space); [GeekWire, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/starcloud-250m-data-center-satellite-network-nvidia/); [BNN Bloomberg, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/company-news/2026/08/21/orbital-data-center-startup-starcloud-valued-at-us23-billion-in-latest-funding/)). GeekWire calls the August round a Series A extension and Wikipedia (as used in note 07) a Series B; the amounts agree. On 3 February 2026 Starcloud applied to the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC, which licenses the radio spectrum US satellites use) for a constellation of up to **88,000 satellites** [A as a filing, D as a plan] ([Wikipedia: Starcloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starcloud)); TechCrunch reported "80,000", a discrepancy not resolved ([TechCrunch, 11 Feb 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/11/why-the-economics-of-orbital-ai-are-so-brutal/)). Inference (note 01): the valuation doubled in five months with no new hardware in orbit, a capital-markets signal driven by the SpaceX/xAI story rather than an engineering milestone.

The roadmap keeps changing. Starcloud-2 was to launch in October 2026 with about 100 times Starcloud-1's power, H100s and Blackwell B200s [B] ([GeekWire, 22 Dec 2025](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/starcloud-power-training-ai-space/)); by March 2026 it was due "later in 2026" with a Blackwell GPU, an AWS server blade, a bitcoin-mining computer and "the largest deployable radiator on a private satellite" [B] ([TechCrunch, 30 Mar 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/30/starcloud-raises-170-million-series-ato-build-data-centers-in-space)). **No Starcloud-2 launch had been confirmed by 5 October 2026.** Starcloud-3 grew from a 2-tonne, 100 kW craft (December 2025) to a **3-tonne, 200 kW craft to be launched on Starship, with commercial access in 2028-2029 and power at "$0.05 per kW/hour" contingent on $500/kg launch** [B for the plan, D for the cost] ([TechCrunch, 30 Mar 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/30/starcloud-raises-170-million-series-ato-build-data-centers-in-space)), and it is to carry NVIDIA's Space-1 Vera Rubin module ([GeekWire, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/starcloud-250m-data-center-satellite-network-nvidia/)). Johnston's own hedge, "If it ends up being delayed, we'll just carry on launching the smaller versions on Falcon 9", concedes the business case needs Starship.

Customers so far are partnerships, not paid capacity. Crusoe agreed on 22 October 2025 to run its cloud on a Starcloud satellite launching in late 2026, offering "limited GPU capacity" from early 2027 [A for the partnership, B for the capacity] ([Crusoe, 22 Oct 2025](https://www.crusoe.ai/resources/newsroom/crusoe-to-become-first-cloud-operator-in-space-through-partnership-with-starcloud); [DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/crusoe-to-deploy-in-starcloud-satellite-data-center-in-late-2026-offer-limited-gpu-capacity-in-space-from-2027)). Johnston has described a Crusoe "10 gigawatt contract (2032-2037) at 3¢/kWh" worth "$13.1 billion"; the arithmetic works, but no binding terms are public, so it stays [D] ([GeekWire, 22 Dec 2025](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/starcloud-power-training-ai-space/)). On 26 May 2026 SpaceX contracted to supply Starlink laser terminals for Starcloud's next 25 satellites [A per Wikipedia, not independently verified; Unite.AI gives 50+ terminals and 25 Gbps links] ([Unite.AI, Aug 2026](https://www.unite.ai/starcloud-raises-250m-series-a-extension-at-2-3b-valuation/)). On 30 September 2026 Firefly signed Starcloud to fly an SC-1L compute processor in lunar orbit on Firefly's third lunar mission, no earlier than 2028, contract value undisclosed [A for the contract, B for the mission] ([Firefly/GlobeNewswire, 30 Sep 2026](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/30/3371832/0/en/firefly-aerospace-signs-starcloud-as-commercial-customer-demonstrating-data-center-capabilities-around-the-moon.html)). The big claims are hype [D]: a 5 GW station with solar and cooling panels about 4 km by 4 km, "10x lower energy costs", and Johnston's "In 10 years, nearly all new data centers will be being built in outer space" ([NVIDIA blog, 15 Oct 2025](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/starcloud/)). Starcloud's own September 2024 whitepaper actually claims about 20x, not 10x, by leaving out the GPUs and the satellites' manufacture and assuming launch at about $30/kg (section 2.5) ([Starcloud whitepaper v1.03, Sep 2024](https://starcloudinc.github.io/wp.pdf)); the "10x" headline was not found in the whitepaper text (note 08).

#### Google and NVIDIA: the credible version and the arms dealer

Google is doing the most credible version, because it publishes measurements. Project Suncatcher, announced on 4 November 2025, put a Trillium (v6e) TPU in a 67 MeV proton beam: its high-bandwidth memory (HBM, the fast memory stacked beside AI chips) showed irregularities only after 2 krad(Si), against an expected shielded five-year dose of about 750 rad(Si), and there were **no hard failures up to 15 krad(Si)**, the maximum tested [A, lab] ([Google Research blog, 4 Nov 2025](https://research.google/blog/exploring-a-space-based-scalable-ai-infrastructure-system-design/)). A bench test sent 800 Gbps each way (1.6 Tbps total) through one pair of optical transceivers [A, lab], and the modelled cluster is 81 satellites flying 100-200 m apart within a 1 km radius at 650 km [C] ([Suncatcher paper, arXiv 2511.19468](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.19468)). Google ties viability to launch falling to "less than $200/kg by the mid-2030s" [C]. **On 1 October 2026 the first Suncatcher prototype, built with Planet, launched on SpaceX's Transporter-18 and is "operating as expected"** [A], and a peer-reviewed paper appeared in *Joule* ([Google blog, Oct 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-research/project-suncatcher-prototype/)). Futurum adds, though Google's post does not say it, that the satellite carries four Trillium TPUs, will operate for a year and deorbit within six, and that a two-satellite launch in 2027 will test optical links between satellites [B] ([Futurum, 25 Sep 2026](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/project-suncatcher-prepares-to-launch-tpus-is-google-ahead-in-the-orbital-ai-race/)). The original plan was "two prototype satellites by early 2027"; it has become one prototype now and a pair in 2027. The Wall Street Journal reported in May 2026 that Google was in talks with SpaceX and other providers to launch Suncatcher [B, reported] ([Tom's Hardware, 13 May 2026](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/google-reportedly-in-talks-with-spacex-to-launch-its-orbital-data-centers-partnership-could-mark-a-historic-turning-point-and-boost-upcoming-ipo)).

NVIDIA sells to everyone. At its GTC conference on 16-17 March 2026 it announced a **Space-1 Vera Rubin module** promising "up to 25x more AI compute" than an H100, not yet commercially available [B], and named Aetherflux, Axiom Space, Kepler, Planet, Sophia Space and Starcloud as users of its space hardware ([Payload, 17 Mar 2026](https://payloadspace.com/nvidia-announces-new-hardware-to-unlock-orbital-data-centers/)). Jensen Huang declared "Space computing, the final frontier, has arrived" while admitting that "in space there's no conduction, there's no convection, there's just radiation". Around 4 August 2026 NVIDIA agreed to design the compute payload (Rubin GPUs and Vera CPUs) for SpaceX's Starmind AI1 satellites [B]; the stake it took in xAI's $20 billion round in January 2026 converted into SpaceX stock after the merger [A] ([Tech Startups, 4 Aug 2026](https://techstartups.com/2026/08/04/nvidia-partners-with-spacex-to-build-starmind-ai-orbital-data-centers-in-space/); [Motley Fool, 6 Aug 2026](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/08/06/just-announced-spacex-and-nvidia-teaming-up-on-new-orbital-ai-data-center/)).

#### SpaceX/xAI and Blue Origin: filings, a merger and an IPO, but no compute hardware flown

SpaceX has turned orbital compute into a licence application, a corporate structure and a stock-market story without yet flying any compute hardware. Its FCC application SAT-LOA-20260108-00016, filed on 8 January 2026 and accepted for filing on 4 February 2026, covers **up to 1,000,000 satellites** at 500-2,000 km, describes itself as "the first step towards becoming a Kardashev II-level civilization", and asks the FCC to waive its processing-round, deployment-milestone and surety-bond rules [A, filing] ([FCC Public Notice DA 26-113, 4 Feb 2026](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-113A1.pdf)); outlets variously date the filing 30 January or February, but the file number shows 8 January. It claims that once Starship flies, 1 million tonnes a year at 100 kW per tonne would add "100 GW" of AI compute a year, without hardware or mass details [D] ([DCD, 31 Jan 2026](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/spacex-files-for-million-satellite-orbital-ai-data-center-megaconstellation/)). Musk at Davos in January 2026 called orbital data centres a "no brainer" achievable within "two to three years" [D] ([Gizmodo, 22 Apr 2026](https://gizmodo.com/trillion-dollar-flip-flop-spacex-says-orbital-data-centers-may-never-make-money-2000749584)). SpaceX acquired xAI on 2 February 2026 in an all-stock deal valuing the combination at about $1.25 trillion [A] ([Via Satellite, 2 Feb 2026](https://www.satellitetoday.com/connectivity/2026/02/02/spacex-acquires-xai-to-pursue-orbital-data-center-constellation/)) and listed on Nasdaq as SPCX in June 2026 (section 2.8). Its own prospectus (the S-1 registration statement filed for an IPO) warns that "Our initiatives to develop orbital AI compute and in-orbit, lunar, and interplanetary industrialization are in early stages, involve significant technical complexity and unproven technologies, and may not achieve commercial viability" [A] ([Gizmodo, 22 Apr 2026](https://gizmodo.com/trillion-dollar-flip-flop-spacex-says-orbital-data-centers-may-never-make-money-2000749584)). The S-1 also reportedly concedes that SpaceX cannot currently secure enough chips domestically [A, via press] ([Tom's Hardware, 9 Jun 2026](https://tomshardware.com/tech-industry/spacex-details-its-ai1-compute-satellite)), mentions a "Terafab" chip-production venture with Tesla [A, mention] ([Yahoo Finance on the S-1, May 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/article/spacex-files-ipo-prospectus-offering-a-peek-into-its-finances-205406189.html)), and sets a "long-term goal" of launching 100 GW of compute a year [D] ([iTiger summary of SemiAnalysis, 4 Jun 2026, secondary](https://www.itiger.com/news/1162677489)).

The product is **Starmind AI1**, and its specification is not frozen. June 2026 reports gave about 120 kW average and 150 kW peak computing power, a ~70 m solar array, ~600 km altitude, 70 kW per tonne, up to 110 m² of deployable liquid radiators, first prototypes in early 2027 and volume production by the end of 2027 at a new factory [B] ([Notebookcheck, 24 Jun 2026](https://www.notebookcheck.net/SpaceX-names-its-AI-Orbital-Data-Center-satellite-constellation.1328148.0.html); [Tom's Hardware, 9 Jun 2026](https://tomshardware.com/tech-industry/spacex-details-its-ai1-compute-satellite)); August reports gave 250 kW peak and 175 kW average, a 75 m wingspan and 75 kW per tonne [B] ([Tech Startups, 4 Aug 2026](https://techstartups.com/2026/08/04/nvidia-partners-with-spacex-to-build-starmind-ai-orbital-data-centers-in-space/)). No primary SpaceX specification sheet was found, and even the August figure misses the filing's 100 kW per tonne. Before AI1, SpaceX plans to put compute on existing Starlink satellites as "canary" tests [B] ([DCD, Jun 2026](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/spacex-ipo-musks-firm-set-to-launch-first-orbital-data-center-ai1-satellites-in-2027-will-put-compute-on-starlink-craft/)), and Musk said in June that "A lot of this is technology we've already made for the Starlink V3 satellites" [B/C] ([MarketScreener, Jun 2026](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/ahead-of-spacex-ipo-musk-says-ai-satellites-will-use-mostly-existing-technology-ce7f5dd3dc8bf72d)). Tom's Hardware reported in May 2026 that SpaceX had "struck a partnership" with Anthropic that could cover "multiple gigawatts of orbital AI compute capacity"; its terms were not verified [B, reported] ([Tom's Hardware, 13 May 2026](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/google-reportedly-in-talks-with-spacex-to-launch-its-orbital-data-centers-partnership-could-mark-a-historic-turning-point-and-boost-upcoming-ipo)).

Jeff Bezos predicted in October 2025 that gigawatt-scale data centres would be built in space within 10-20 years, while acknowledging launch cost, failures and maintenance [C/D] ([Military & Aerospace Electronics, 6 Oct 2025](https://militaryaerospace.com/home/news/55321161/jeff-bezos-predicts-space-based-data-centers-within-two-decades)). Blue Origin announced a roughly 5,400-satellite enterprise network called TeraWave in January 2026 [B] ([Wikipedia: Space-based data center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-based_data_center)) and in March 2026 filed "Project Sunrise", **up to 51,600 satellites for space-based AI data centres, asking the FCC to waive its deployment milestones** [A, filing] ([NASASpaceFlight, 21 Mar 2026](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/2026/03/blue-new-glenn-manufacturing-data-ambitions/)). Inference (note 01): a company that asks for milestone waivers does not expect to build at scale on the normal schedule, and both giant filings are partly land grabs, since priority over orbital shells and spectrum goes to whoever files first.

#### Smaller Western players: edge nodes, studies and letters of intent

Eric Schmidt took control of Relativity Space as CEO in March 2025 and confirmed with a one-word reply that the purchase was about orbital data centres; no funded programme has been disclosed [A for the stake, D for intent] ([TechSpot, 5 May 2025](https://www.techspot.com/news/107801-former-google-ceo-eric-schmidt-wants-put-data.html)). Orbital, a five-month-old Los Angeles startup, filed on 24 June 2026 for up to 100,000 satellites delivering about 10 GW [A, filing] ([Broadband Breakfast, 9 Jul 2026](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/orbital-seeks-fcc-approval-for-100-000-satellite-data-center-constellation/)). Aetherflux, founded by Robinhood co-founder Baiju Bhatt with about $60 million raised, promises a first "Galactic Brain" node by the first quarter of 2027 [B]; whether its 2026 power-beaming demonstration satellite flew is unconfirmed ([DCD, 16 Dec 2025](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/aetherflux-orbital-data-center-to-be-operational-by-q1-2027/)). Axiom Space put two orbital data-centre nodes into orbit on 11 January 2026, hosted on Kepler Communications' optical relay satellites with 2.5 Gbps laser links, and commits to more only "as demand materializes" [A] ([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/axiom-space-to-launch-orbital-data-centers-on-kepler-satellites/); [Introl, 20 Jan 2026, aggregator](https://introl.com/blog/orbital-data-center-nodes-launch-space-computing-infrastructure-january-2026)); the Texas Space Commission gave it $5.5 million in May 2025 [A] ([Axiom Space](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/texas-space-commission-fuels-orbital-data-center)). Sophia Space raised a $10 million seed on 5 March 2026 [A] ([Hogan Lovells, 5 Mar 2026](https://www.hlc.com/en/news/hogan-lovells-advises-sophia-space-as-it-closes)); its headline "$300 million" asset financing of 14 September 2026 is explicitly a **non-binding letter of support** [B at best] ([ePlaneAI, 14 Sep 2026](https://www.eplaneai.com/es/news/sophia-space-secures-300-million-lease-to-finance-first-orbital-computing-constellation)). Luxembourg's Edge Aerospace flew a demonstrator in March 2026 and won an ESA "Space Cloud" study contract on 5 May 2026 [A] ([Payload, 5 May 2026](https://payloadspace.com/esa-selects-edge-aerospace-to-study-orbital-data-center-concepts/)).

The sharpest sceptics are insiders. OpenAI's Sam Altman said on 22 February 2026 that "the idea with the current landscape of putting data centers in space is ridiculous" and that "Orbital data centers are not something that is going to matter at scale this decade", citing launch costs and GPUs that "break a lot still" [C] ([Tom's Hardware, 22 Feb 2026](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/sam-altman-fires-back-at-elon-musks-proposal-for-space-based-data-centers-says-orbiting-data-centers-ridiculous-for-now-cites-high-failure-rates-and-cost-as-primary-limiters)). AWS chief executive Matt Garman called them "just not economical" in February 2026: "There are not enough rockets to launch a million satellites yet" [C] ([DCD, 4 Feb 2026](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/amazon-web-services-ceo-says-were-pretty-far-from-having-a-million-space-data-centers-calls-them-just-not-economical/)), contradicting Amazon's founder. No 2025-2026 orbital-compute programme or statement was found for Microsoft or Meta. Inference (note 01): the split follows business models, with launch sellers and chip vendors bullish and companies buying power for ground data centres sceptical.

#### China, Europe and Japan: China leads on deployed count, not on chip class

China operates the largest orbital-compute constellation, though not the most powerful. ADA Space and Zhejiang Lab launched **12 "Three-Body" satellites on 14 May 2025**, each rated at 744 TOPS (trillion operations per second; Yicai writes "teraflops", so the unit is uncertain), about 5 POPS combined, with laser links, 30 TB of storage and an 8-billion-parameter on-board model [A] ([Yicai Global/Xinhua, 14 May 2025](https://www.yicaiglobal.com/news/china-launches-space-computing-satellites-with-ai-into-orbit)). Alibaba's Qwen3 model was deployed on them in January 2026 [A], with further batches planned for 2026 and 2,800 satellites eventually [B] ([China in Space, 4 Feb 2026](https://www.china-in-space.com/p/potential-space-data-centers-attracts)); Xinhua-sourced coverage instead gives "1,000+" satellites [B] ([eHangzhou/Xinhua, 14 Feb 2026](https://www.ehangzhou.gov.cn/2026-02/14/c_296817.htm)). This is edge-inference class, not H100 or TPU class (inference, note 01). A SatNews story attributing a "Three-Body" test to CASIC with three CubeSats contradicts the primary facts and is discounted. Beijing's Astro-future Institute announced on 28 November 2025 a plan for 16 orbital data centres at 700-800 km with a reported "16 GW", phased as tests in 2025-2027, cost parity with the ground in 2028-2030 and large AI-training facilities in 2031-2035 [B/D] ([China Daily, 28 Nov 2025](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202511/28/WS6928ff2fa310d6866eb2bde4.html)). The institute raised at least 140 million yuan (about $20 million) [A] ([China in Space, 4 Feb 2026](https://www.china-in-space.com/p/potential-space-data-centers-attracts)), and an associated startup, Orbital Chenguang, reportedly holds about 57.7 billion yuan (about $8.4 billion) of state-bank **credit lines, which are not drawn capital** [B, secondary] ([CarbonCredits.com, 27 Apr 2026](https://carboncredits.com/chinas-8-4b-orbital-data-center-push-sets-up-space-based-ai-showdown-with-spacex/)). China's international (ITU) satellite filings are reported as 96,714 satellites (CarbonCredits) or a 200,000-satellite constellation (Reuters, via [Wikipedia: Space-based data center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-based_data_center)); the figures conflict and whether each is for computing or communications is unclear.

Europe has a feasibility study, not a programme. Thales Alenia Space's €2 million, 16-month ASCEND study for the European Commission (results 27 June 2024) found space data centres feasible at about 1,400 km only with a launcher "ten times less emissive" than today's, targeting 10 MW by 2036 and 1 GW by 2050 [C] ([CNBC via NBC Washington, 27 Jun 2024](https://nbcwashington.com/news/business/money-report/europe-wants-to-send-data-centers-into-space-study-says-its-possible/3651049); [Thales Alenia Space, 27 Jun 2024](https://thalesaleniaspace.com/en/press-releases/thales-alenia-space-reveals-results-ascend-feasibility-study-space-data-centers-0)). Japan's NTT and SKY Perfect JSAT planned a space computing network in 2021 with launches in 2025 [B as of 2021]; its current status is unknown ([DCD, 19 May 2021](https://datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/ntt-and-sky-perfect-jsat-to-launch-space-data-center-in-2025)). No Indian or UAE programme was verified.

#### Lunar "data centres": a backup drive on a lander that fell over

Lonestar's "Freedom" payload, 1 kg carrying 8 TB of solid-state storage, rode Intuitive Machines' IM-2 lander, which landed at Mons Mouton on 6 March 2025, **tipped over into a crater and ended its mission about 13 hours later** [A] ([IEEE Spectrum, 25 Feb 2025](https://spectrum.ieee.org/data-center-on-the-moon); [Wikipedia: IM-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IM-2)). Lonestar still describes it as "a first-of-its-kind data center on the Moon" ([DCD, 17 Apr 2026](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/lonestar-announces-starvault-data-storage-platform-extends-agreement-for-construction-with-sidus-space/)), but surface operations are unverified and at best minimal (Phison's blog dates the landing to 4 March, which is wrong). Lonestar raised $6.6 million in January 2026 under a new chief executive, Stephen Eisele [A] ([Techleap/Dealroom, Jan 2026](https://finder.techleap.nl/news/feed/lonestar-data-raises-6-6m-for-moon-data-centers-names-new-ceo-1)), reports a "$120 million" agreement with Sidus Space for six data-storage satellites at the Earth-Moon L1 point in 2027-2030 [A as reported, B for delivery; a contract 18 times the money raised implies staged or contingent terms], and planned its first StarVault storage payload in Earth orbit for October 2026 on Sidus's LizzieSat-4 [B; launch unconfirmed] ([DCD, 17 Apr 2026](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/lonestar-announces-starvault-data-storage-platform-extends-agreement-for-construction-with-sidus-space/)). Inference (note 01): Lonestar's real business is sovereign disaster-recovery storage in orbit, and computing near the Moon only makes sense for data born there, as in Starcloud's Firefly contract; nobody credible proposes AI training on the Moon.

Space computing itself is not new. HPE's Spaceborne Computer-1 ran two off-the-shelf servers on the International Space Station (ISS) for 615 days from 2017, protected by software rather than shielding, and Spaceborne Computer-2 followed in 2021 with links to Microsoft's Azure cloud [A] ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/hewlett-packard-enterprises-spaceborne-computer-returns-to-earth/); [HPE, 11 Feb 2021](https://hpe.com/us/en/newsroom/press-release/2021/02/hewlett-packard-enterprise-accelerates-space-exploration-with-first-ever-in-space-commercial-edge-computing-and-artificial-intelligence-capabilities.html)); ESA's Phi-Sat-1 used an AI chip to discard cloudy images on board in 2020 [A] ([Ubotica](https://www.ubotica.com/news/successful-launch-of-ground-breaking-ai-enabled-earth-observation-satellite)). That edge-processing lineage is the only proven business case; selling bulk training compute from orbit has no precedent (inference, note 01).

No credible projection found supports gigawatt-scale orbital compute before 2035. Forethought's scenarios are far more aggressive and conflict with that baseline: about 4 GW in orbit by 2031 in its slow case and about 100 GW by the end of 2030 in a fast case that needs a reusable Starship by 2027 and 10,000+ launches by 2030 [C] ([Forethought, 22 May 2026](https://forethought.org/research/will-we-really-put-data-centers-in-space)). The gap reflects Forethought assuming that Starship cadence arrives on time; neither view has flight evidence for the 100 kW+ satellites both depend on.

### 2.2 Physics and engineering: radiators and mass, not sunlight, set the bill

An orbital data centre is a solar farm, a cooling plant and a server hall bolted together and launched, with no technician ever visiting. Sunlight is the easy part. The hard parts are getting rid of heat, carrying the mass of arrays and radiators, living with radiation and failures without repairs, and moving data between satellites.

#### Power: abundant sunlight, heavy and expensive arrays

Above the atmosphere sunlight delivers about 1,361 W/m², but an object in an ordinary low orbit averages about 800 W/m² over a day because it spends part of each orbit in Earth's shadow [A physics, C analysis] ([SemiAnalysis, 3 Jun 2026](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/to-boldly-go-the-case-for-space-datacenters)). That is why every serious design uses a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit: Google says a panel there can "receive up to 8× more solar energy per year than a panel located on Earth at mid-latitude" [C] ([Suncatcher paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.19468)), and Starcloud claims a capacity factor (share of the year at full output) above 95% against 24% for median US ground solar [C/D] ([Starcloud whitepaper, Sep 2024](https://starcloudinc.github.io/wp.pdf)). The Breakthrough Institute's claim that orbit harnesses only "25% more useful solar radiation" ([Breakthrough Institute, 17 Feb 2026](https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/energy/data-centers-wont-be-in-space-anytime-soon)) is not a contradiction: it compares peak brightness, while Google and Starcloud compare energy over a year including night and weather. Dawn-dusk orbits are not eclipse-free; ESA's PROBA2 in such an orbit has an eclipse season of about 80 days a year (November-January) with shadows of up to 18 minutes per orbit [A] ([ESA PROBA2](https://proba2.sidc.be/about/launch)). Inference (note 02): an ordinary 400-650 km orbit spends about 36-39% of each orbit in shadow at worst, a dawn-dusk orbit about 1-2% of the year, and GEO about 1% (up to 69 minutes a day in seasons around the equinoxes).

The weak link is the mass and cost of the arrays. Flown arrays deliver about 70-140 W per kg: Redwire's ROSA range runs from 3.3 kW for 36 kg (about 92 W/kg) to 37 kW for 521 kg (about 71 W/kg), and NASA's 2026 small-spacecraft survey finds most operational missions near 30 W/kg with a maximum of about 200 W/kg [A] ([Redwire](https://rdw.com/product-archive/power/rosa/); [NASA SST-SOA Power, 7 May 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/power-subsystems/)). SpaceX's AI1 uses a 150 kW array at 250 W/m², about 600 m² [B] ([Tom's Hardware, 9 Jun 2026](https://tomshardware.com/tech-industry/spacex-details-its-ai1-compute-satellite)). Lighter cells exist in the lab: Surrey reports silicon heterojunction cells at 920-1,000 W/kg against 455-505 W/kg for triple-junction space cells, though silicon makes about 28% less power per area and triple-junction cells are about 2.6 times more radiation-resistant [C] ([University of Surrey, 23 Sep 2026](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/silicon-solar-cells-could-cut-satellite-power-costs-90-cent)); Caltech showed a thin-film gallium arsenide device at 500 W/kg [C, lab] ([Caltech, Jul 2024](https://spw.aerospace.org/files/2024/07/5_EG1_P.-Jahelka_Presentation.pdf)); Starcloud assumes thin-film silicon above 1,000 W/kg, never flown at scale [D]. Prices disagree: Caltech puts space-grade III-V cells at "~$100/W" and Starlink-style silicon at "$0.5/W" [A/C], while Surrey puts space-grade III-V at $250-450/W against $0.28-0.39/W for terrestrial cells [A prices/C]; the basis (bare cell or finished array, year) is not defined in either. Starcloud's "$0.03 per watt" is [D]. Starcloud's own CEO concedes that radiation wears silicon panels down so that "After five or six years, the dollars per kilowatt-hour doesn't produce a return" [C] ([TechCrunch, 11 Feb 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/11/why-the-economics-of-orbital-ai-are-so-brutal/)). Inference (note 02): the array for 1 MW of computing (about 1.2 MW with margin) weighs about 8-17 t with today's flown technology, about 2.5-4 t at a plausible 300-500 W/kg for thin-film arrays, and about 1.2 t only at Starcloud's cell-level claim; it covers about 3,200-4,000 m².

#### Heat rejection: radiators set the size of the machine

In vacuum there is no air or water to carry heat away; the only exit is radiation from hot panels into space, governed by the Stefan-Boltzmann law (radiated power rises with the fourth power of temperature). As Jensen Huang put it, "in space there's no conduction, there's no convection, there's just radiation" ([Payload, 17 Mar 2026](https://payloadspace.com/nvidia-announces-new-hardware-to-unlock-orbital-data-centers/)). Note 02's idealised calculation (emissivity 0.9, both faces radiating, no heat arriving from Earth or Sun) shows why hotter coolant is the main lever:

| Radiator temperature | Heat rejected (W/m², ideal) | Radiator area per MW (ideal) |
|---|---|---|
| 300 K (27 °C) | 827 | 1,210 m² |
| 330 K (57 °C) | 1,210 | 826 m² |
| 350 K (77 °C) | 1,532 | 653 m² |
| 373 K (100 °C) | 1,976 | 506 m² |

Real radiators lose 15-30% to Earth's infrared glow, reflected sunlight and fin losses (inference, note 02). Starcloud's own figure is 633 W/m² net at 20 °C, about 1,580 m² per MW [C/D] ([Starcloud whitepaper](https://starcloudinc.github.io/wp.pdf)), which the World Economic Forum likens to "a hockey rink" per megawatt, cooling ">1,000 times slower" than water on chips on Earth [C, opinion] ([WEF, 2 Jun 2026](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/06/space-data-centres-cooling/)). The flown benchmark is the ISS: NASA gives its radiators as about 7.0 kg/m², about 125 kW over about 1,300 m² using ammonia at about 300 K, and its proposed MARVL system targets under 4.5 kg/m² for about 4 MW by running liquid metal at 450-550 K [A for ISS, C for MARVL] ([NASA NTRS, Stark et al., 13 Jul 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260005820/downloads/ICES_MARVL_STARK_v3.pdf)). Other sources give the ISS as 70 kW over 422 m² ([Tom's Hardware](https://tomshardware.com/tech-industry/spacex-details-its-ai1-compute-satellite)) or 70 kW over 325 m² at a cost of $340-500 million ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/to-boldly-go-the-case-for-space-datacenters)); the likely cause is counting different radiator sets or faces, so NASA's 7 kg/m² is the safer anchor. Scaling the ISS's whole cold-running system, the WEF piece gets roughly 100 t of radiators per MW, "close to ten times" the ~10 t of the computing racks themselves [C, opinion]; treat that as the pessimistic bound.

SpaceX is betting on hot loops. AI1 is to reject about 120 kW with "up to 110 m²" of deployable liquid radiators [B] ([Tom's Hardware, 9 Jun 2026](https://tomshardware.com/tech-industry/spacex-details-its-ai1-compute-satellite)); inference (note 02): that needs a radiator at no less than about 322 K even in ideal conditions, and in practice coolant at about 340-360 K, which only works if both faces see deep space. Musk argues that raising chip temperatures by 20% in kelvin could "slash radiator mass by half", which needs chips running 50-70 °C hotter than today's limits [D/C] ([WEF](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/06/space-data-centres-cooling/)), and Starcloud says it is working with NVIDIA on higher-temperature hardware [C] ([StartupHub, 6 May 2026](https://www.startuphub.ai/space/starcloud-ceo-space-compute-cheaper-than-earth-in-5-10-years.md)). Former NASA and Google engineer Rory McKinley estimates that 200 kW of H200-class load needs about 531 m² of radiator, "2.6× larger than [the] solar array" [C, blog] ([taranis.ie, updated 10 Jun 2026](https://taranis.ie/datacenters-in-space-are-a-terrible-horrible-no-good-idea/)). Starcloud-2 is to carry the "largest commercial deployable radiator ever sent to space" [B]; no large deployable radiator has flight data yet.

| Radiator areal mass | At ~300 K net (1,580 m²/MW) | At ideal 350 K (653 m²/MW) |
|---|---|---|
| 7 kg/m² (ISS, flown) | 11.1 t/MW | 4.6 t/MW |
| 4.5 kg/m² (NASA MARVL target) | 7.1 t/MW | 2.9 t/MW |
| 2 kg/m² (aggressive assumption) | 3.2 t/MW | 1.3 t/MW |

Inference (note 02): add an unknown 20-50% for pumps, loops and heat exchangers; a plausible 2031-2035 figure is about 3-8 t of radiator per MW, against proponents' 1-3 t.

#### Radiation: the one measured result is encouraging

Radiation harms chips in three ways: single-event upsets (a particle flips a bit), latch-up (a particle triggers a short circuit that can burn out a path) and total ionising dose (slow cumulative damage that raises power draw and lowers speed) [C] ([taranis.ie](https://taranis.ie/datacenters-in-space-are-a-terrible-horrible-no-good-idea/)). The only measured data for a modern AI accelerator is Google's beam test: beyond the dose results above, it measured silent data corruption (wrong answers with no error flag) at about "one event/17 rad", equivalent to roughly "1 per 3 million inferences", against an expected "∼150 rad(Si)/year" behind about 10 mm of aluminium shielding [A for the beam data, C for the orbit extrapolation] ([Suncatcher paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.19468)); one summary instead gives "~1 failure per 10 million inferences" ([Watkins, secondary](https://www.jaredwatkins.com/research/datacenters/orbital-compute/google-project-suncatcher/)). HPE's off-the-shelf servers survived 615 days on the ISS, passing through the South Atlantic Anomaly (a radiation hot spot) more than 6,800 times [A] ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/hewlett-packard-enterprises-spaceborne-computer-returns-to-earth/)). The worry is training reliability: even on the ground, Meta's Llama 3 training on H100s suffered 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days [A], and the Breakthrough Institute argues orbital radiation would add "nigh-constant systemwide disruptions" [C] ([Breakthrough Institute](https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/energy/data-centers-wont-be-in-space-anytime-soon)). SemiAnalysis models 95% "radiation availability" in orbit [C], and the US Government Accountability Office (GAO) notes radiation "can corrupt data unpredictably and degrade hardware" [A, report] ([GAO-26-109012, Apr 2026](https://www.gao.gov/assets/890/885431.pdf)). Starcloud assumes "1 kg of shielding per kW of compute" [D/C] ([Starcloud whitepaper](https://starcloudinc.github.io/wp.pdf)). Inference (note 02): 10 mm of aluminium weighs 27 kg per m² of shielded face, so shielding per kW falls as containers grow; Google's figures imply roughly 9 silent errors per accelerator per year (assuming the rate is per device, which the paper does not make fully explicit), harmless for inference with retries but costly for tightly synchronised training; and the memory, not the logic, is the weak link, with about 2.7 times margin over a five-year dose. No flight error data from Starcloud-1 and no latch-up data for Blackwell or Rubin chips were found.

#### Servicing, lifetime and obsolescence: designed to be thrown away

Nobody can swap a GPU in orbit. Servicing heritage is limited to Northrop Grumman's two Mission Extension Vehicles in GEO, which have serviced three satellites and added more than 10 years of combined life, and its Mission Robotic Vehicle with two robotic arms, launched on 22 July 2026 for GEO [A] ([ASDNews, 22 Jul 2026](https://asdnews.com/news/aerospace/2026/07/22/ngcs-mission-robotic-vehicle-launches-ushering-new-era-inspace-servicing)). Google notes that on the ground "Failed TPUs are manually replaced", which is "impracticable in space", so designs need "redundant provisioning" [C] ([Suncatcher paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.19468)). SemiAnalysis assumes 20% spare hardware in orbit against 5% cold spares on the ground, where "3-6% of GPUs in a cluster annually suffer failures that require human intervention", and a 5-year orbital life (10 years from 2032) against 15 years for a ground facility [C] ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/to-boldly-go-the-case-for-space-datacenters)); McCalip assumes 9% of GPUs fail each year in orbit [C] ([McCalip calculator](https://andrewmccalip.com/space-datacenters)); Starcloud claims a 10-year design life with 15-year cooling and power systems [D/C]. Meanwhile AI chips used for training have a useful life of 2-3 years and NVIDIA now releases a generation a year [C] ([Breakthrough Institute](https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/energy/data-centers-wont-be-in-space-anytime-soon)), and Starlink satellites last "five to seven years" [A] ([Wikipedia: Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink)). The GAO calls in-space servicing "underdeveloped", which may force frequent decommissioning and add debris [A, report] ([GAO-26-109012](https://www.gao.gov/assets/890/885431.pdf)). Re-entry is already routine: more than three intact satellites or rocket bodies re-entered per day on average in 2025 [A] ([ESA, 14 Sep 2026](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2026)), and Rosenthal cites concern that heavy re-entry traffic could harm the ozone layer [C, blog] ([DSHR](https://blog.dshr.org/2025/12/data-centers-in-spaaaace.html)). Inference (note 02): GPU death and GPU obsolescence arriving at about the same time is roughly the best case; plausible game parameters are a 5-year node life (5-7 years by 2035), 5-10% hard failures a year, 15-25% spares, and no low-orbit servicing before about 2033-2035, since docking-and-swap has never been demonstrated in LEO.

#### Links, latency and workloads: bandwidth, not distance, is the limit

Distance barely matters in low orbit: 600 km is about 2 ms one way (inference, note 02), against about 120 ms one way to GEO [A] ([Wikipedia: Geostationary orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_orbit)) and about 1.4 s to the Moon, which Lonestar acknowledges [A] ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/data-center-on-the-moon)); routing through other satellites adds 30-80 ms one way (SemiAnalysis, via [DiarioBitcoin, 4 Jun 2026, secondary](https://www.diariobitcoin.com/tecnologia/datacenters-espaciales-por-que-la-ia-en-orbita-aun-cuesta-mas-de-4-veces-que-en-la-tierra/)). Correction: note 02's "L1, about 5 s one way" refers to the Sun-Earth L1 point about 1.5 million km away; Lonestar's planned storage satellites sit at the Earth-Moon L1 point, which is much closer (inference; its distance was not sourced in the notes). Rosenthal's claim that 500-700 km altitude makes latency "unsuitable" for AI is weak on the physics [C, blog]. Bandwidth is the real constraint. Starlink's flown lasers run at about 200 Gbps per link across more than 9,000 links, carrying 42 PB a day [A] ([Advanced Television, 2 Feb 2024](https://www.advanced-television.com/2024/02/02/spacex-reveals-starlink-laser-capacity/)), though TechCrunch gives "~100 Gbps maximum" for today's links ([TechCrunch, 11 Feb 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/11/why-the-economics-of-orbital-ai-are-so-brutal/)); Starcloud's contracted terminals give 25 Gbps [A]. Google's training cluster needs "on the order of 10 Tbps" per link between satellites 100-200 m apart, demonstrated only at 1.6 Tbps on a bench [A bench, C design] ([Suncatcher paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.19468)), about 50 times beyond flown links. Laser downlinks have reached 200 Gbps (NASA/MIT TBIRD, 2023), but clouds block lasers, so ground stations sit on mountains [A] ([MIT Lincoln Laboratory](https://www.ll.mit.edu/news/communications-system-achieves-fastest-laser-link-space-yet); [Via Satellite, May 2023](https://www.satellitetoday.com/technology/2023/05/12/nasas-tbird-mission-demonstrates-breaks-its-own-record-with-200-gbps-optical-downlink/)). McKinley's "about 1Gbps reliably" is contradicted by Starlink's flown lasers and looks outdated. SemiAnalysis calls low latency, free cooling, 24-hour sun and no permitting "superficial reasons", and prices space inference at $590 per billion tokens against $135 on the ground (2026) [C] ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/to-boldly-go-the-case-for-space-datacenters)); Breakthrough and Forethought both expect on-satellite edge processing and inference to come first [C] ([Forethought, 22 May 2026](https://forethought.org/research/will-we-really-put-data-centers-in-space)).

| Workload (best fit first; inference, note 02) | Why |
|---|---|
| Processing data born in orbit (Earth observation, radar) | Saves downlink; already a business |
| Batch inference and token generation | Kilobytes in and out; silent errors fixed by retries |
| Fine-tuning and small-model training inside one satellite | Shown on Starcloud-1 |
| Frontier training spread across satellites | Needs multi-terabit links between satellites flying in tight formation; unproven before 2030+ |

A 10 TB model checkpoint takes about 53 minutes over a 25 Gbps link (inference, note 02), so weights and training data are an uplink burden even when outputs are small.

### 2.3 Launch economics: one company sets the price, and it is also the main rival

#### What a kilogram to orbit costs today

A dedicated Falcon 9 lists at $74 million in 2026 and carries 17,500 kg to low orbit with the booster recovered, or 22,800 kg if the booster is thrown away [A] ([Wikipedia: Falcon 9, read 5 Oct 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9); [Motley Fool, 21 Mar 2026](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/03/21/how-much-will-a-spacex-starship-launch-cost/)), which is about $4,230/kg reusable and $3,250/kg expendable (inference, note 03). The list price has risen, not fallen: $54 million (2011), $62 million (2018), $67 million (2022), $74 million (2026) [A, secondary] ([newspaceeconomy.ca, 21 Jun 2026](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/06/21/how-accurate-were-elon-musks-falcon-9-and-starship-kg-claims/)). Shared "rideshare" launches cost $350,000 for up to 50 kg plus $7,000/kg above that under SpaceX's February 2026 price table [A, two secondary sources] ([newspaceeconomy.ca, 27 Feb 2026](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/02/27/spacex-rideshare-pricing-as-of-february-2026-what-it-costs-whats-included-and-how-buyers-budget-a-mission/); [Tom's Hardware, 13 May 2026](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/google-reportedly-in-talks-with-spacex-to-launch-its-orbital-data-centers-partnership-could-mark-a-historic-turning-point-and-boost-upcoming-ipo)). SpaceX's own cost is far lower: estimates put a reused Falcon 9 at $15-28 million per launch [C] ([Wikipedia: Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9)), and analyst Brian Wang's line-item estimate is $10.5-11 million, about $629/kg [C/D] ([NextBigFuture, Feb 2026, secondary](https://www.nextbigfuture.com/2026/02/spacex-falcon-9-true-cost-to-launch-is-about-300-per-pound-which-is-25-of-selling-price-to-customers.html)). Inference (note 03): SpaceX charges roughly 2.6-7 times its cost and prices against the next-best alternative, so a collapse in its costs does not reach outside customers unless competition forces it. The many "today" figures used by analysts ($1,400-3,600/kg) differ because some use list price, some rideshare and some estimated internal cost (note 02). The S-1 shows where SpaceX's money comes from: its Space segment (launch, Dragon and Starship development together) earned $4.086 billion of revenue in 2025 with a $657 million operating loss, cumulative Starship spending is "over $15 billion", and the Starlink connectivity business earned $11.387 billion with $4.423 billion of operating income [A] ([Yahoo Finance on the S-1, May 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/article/spacex-files-ipo-prospectus-offering-a-peek-into-its-finances-205406189.html)); note 03 cautions that the segment mixes NASA services, Starship R&D and outside launches, so it says nothing about the cost of a single launch.

Competitors are years behind on reuse and do not undercut on price. Blue Origin's New Glenn lifts 45 t with a reused booster, with Blue Origin's own operating-cost estimate of $68-110 million per flight [A spec, C cost], about $1,500-2,450/kg if full (inference), and a larger 9x4 variant for more than 70 t "as early as 2027" [B] ([Wikipedia: New Glenn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Glenn)). ULA flew 6 launches in 2025, targets 18-22 in 2026, and has a backlog of more than 80 missions dominated by national-security and Amazon satellite launches [A/B] ([Spaceflight Now, 11 Feb 2026](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/02/11/ula-sets-sights-on-ramping-up-launch-cadence-in-2026/)). Rocket Lab's Neutron is due to fly first in late 2026 with reported risk of slipping to 2027 [B] ([Kapitalmarktexperten, Aug 2026, secondary](https://www.kapitalmarktexperten.de/rocket-lab-234-millionen-umsatz-neutron-auf-q4-2026/)). China's LandSpace Zhuque-3 lifts 8 t reusable (upgrade planned to 18.3 t) [A/B] ([Wikipedia: Zhuque-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhuque-3)). For the long view, NASA's H. W. Jones (2018) shows list price per kg falling from about $61,700 on the Space Shuttle to $2,720 on Falcon 9 and $1,410 on Falcon Heavy (2018 dollars, list price over maximum payload), a twentyfold cut [A, derived] ([NASA NTRS, ICES-2018-81](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001093/downloads/20200001093.pdf)).

#### Starship: orbit reached, reuse not yet, and one real price

Starship V3 first reached orbit and **deployed 26 working Starlink V3 satellites (about 2 t each) on Flight 14, 28 September 2026** [A] ([Notebookcheck, 28 Sep 2026](https://www.notebookcheck.net/SpaceX-Starship-flight-14-launches-first-Starlink-V3-satellites-for-gigabit-downloads.1410436.0.html)). The record before that was mixed: ships were lost on Flights 7, 8 and 9 in 2025, Flights 10 and 11 met their goals, three boosters have been caught by the launch tower, and the first V3 flights in May and July 2026 deployed payloads but lost or hard-landed their boosters [A] ([Wikipedia: SpaceX Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship); [Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-targets-july-16-for-starship-flight-13-reveals-what-went-wrong-on-previous-launch); [Starlust, Jul 2026](https://starlust.org/lucky-flight-13-space-x-finally-executes-2nd-launch-of-starship-v-3-achieves-softest-splashdown/)). Sources disagree on whether Flight 12 reached orbit; Flight 14 is widely described as the first orbital flight. **As of early October 2026 no ship has been caught or reused and no ship-to-ship propellant transfer has happened** [A, negative] ([Wikipedia: Starship HLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_HLS)); Flight 15, no earlier than 19 October 2026, is reported to attempt the first ship catch [B, secondary only] ([Basenor, secondary](https://www.basenor.com/blogs/news/starship-flight-14-captured-on-film-flight-15-is-next)). The V3 version is rated at 100 t to low orbit reusable, with a later version estimated at about 200 t [B/C] ([Wikipedia: SpaceX Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship)).

On price, Musk's claims have a poor record: about $2 million per mission (2019) and "less than $10 million all-in within two to three years" (2022), implying $7-100/kg, have not been met, and SpaceX has published no Starship price card [D for the claims, A for the absence] ([newspaceeconomy.ca, 21 Jun 2026](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/06/21/how-accurate-were-elon-musks-falcon-9-and-starship-kg-claims/)). **The only contracted Starship price found is $90 million for one 2029 launch of Voyager's Starlab station**, disclosed in Voyager's 10-K [A] ([Motley Fool, 21 Mar 2026](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/03/21/how-much-will-a-spacex-starship-launch-cost/)); that is about $600-900/kg if the rocket is full (inference, note 03). Google's credible optimistic case is "≲$200/kg by the mid-2030s", which assumes a 20% price drop for every doubling of cumulative mass launched and needs about 370,000 t of extra cumulative payload, roughly 180 Starship launches a year [C] ([Suncatcher paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.19468)). Note 03's suggested third-party price path (inference) is the one the design model uses:

| Period | Third-party Starship-class price to LEO (inference, note 03) |
|---|---|
| 2027-2028 | Limited availability; about $600-1,500/kg where offered |
| 2029-2030 | About $500-900/kg, anchored on the $90M 2029 contract |
| 2031-2033 | About $300-600/kg if ship reuse works and a competitor appears |
| 2034-2035 | Optimistic below $200/kg; base $250-500/kg; bear $800+/kg if SpaceX keeps its pricing power or reuse disappoints |

SpaceX's internal cost for its own constellation could be 5-20 times below that third-party price (inference, note 03); McCalip calls vertical integration "the whole ballgame" ([McCalip calculator](https://andrewmccalip.com/space-datacenters)).

#### Cadence: what a gigawatt in orbit would require

In 2025 the world flew about 325 orbital launches (BryceTech; SpaceNews counts 324, McDowell 329 attempts) and put about 2,700 t of spacecraft in orbit; SpaceX flew 165 (about 51%), 122 of them for its own Starlink, and launched 85% of all satellites [A] ([Via Satellite on BryceTech, 10 Apr 2026](https://satellitetoday.com/launch/2026/04/10/brycetech-report-shows-spacex-accounted-for-50-of-launches-in-2025/)). With Falcon carrying an estimated 2,400 t ([Wikipedia: List of Falcon launches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Falcon_9_and_Falcon_Heavy_launches)), SpaceX's share of mass is roughly 85-90% (inference, note 03). China flew 92 launches [A] ([Global Times via GlobalSecurity, 3 Jan 2026](https://www.globalsecurity.org/space/library/news/2026/space-260103-globaltimes01.htm)). By 11 September 2026 Falcon had flown 106 times without a failure, Long March 43 times with 2 failures and New Glenn once (a failure) [A] ([Wikipedia: 2026 in spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_in_spaceflight)); one Falcon booster has flown 37 times ([Spaceflight Now, Aug 2026](https://spaceflightnow.com/launch/falcon-9-starlink-10-49/)).

| 1 GW of orbital compute at... (inference, note 03) | Mass | Launches | Launch bill |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 t/MW (SpaceX's filing claim) | 10,000 t, 3.7x 2025 global upmass | ~100 Starship flights at 100 t | $2B at $200/kg; $6-9B at $600-900/kg; $42B at Falcon 9 list; $70B at rideshare rates |
| 20-50 t/MW (first-generation designs) | 20,000-50,000 t | 200-500 Starship flights | 2-5 times the above |

Musk's 1 million tonnes a year is about 370 times 2025's global upmass and roughly 18 Starship launches a day [D] ([Leon Liao Substack, secondary](https://leonliao.substack.com/p/what-scale-can-spacexs-space-based)); Forethought's fast scenario needs the US launch cap raised from about 145 to more than 5,000 a year [C] ([Forethought, 22 May 2026](https://forethought.org/research/will-we-really-put-data-centers-in-space)). Inference (note 03): a third-party builder in 2031-2035 could plausibly secure lift for 0.1-1 GW a year, and only with Starship access.

#### Who controls launch

SpaceX controls about half of all launches and most of the mass, flies its own Starlink on three-quarters of its launches, has merged with xAI and has filed for a million orbital data-centre satellites, so **a third-party orbital-compute builder buys lift from its largest competitor** (inference, note 03). SpaceX also has reasons to sell: heavy Starship sunk costs, a public listing and reported launch talks with Google and others [B, reported] ([Tom's Hardware, 13 May 2026](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/google-reportedly-in-talks-with-spacex-to-launch-its-orbital-data-centers-partnership-could-mark-a-historic-turning-point-and-boost-upcoming-ipo)). Regulators have trimmed SpaceX before: it asked for about 30,000 second-generation Starlinks and received 7,500 in 2022 and 7,500 more in 2026 [A] ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/science/space/spacex-wants-to-launch-a-constellation-of-a-million-satellites-to-power-ai-needs-175607771.html)). National-security launches take priority on some vehicles; the 2025 Phase 3 Lane 2 awards split about $13.7 billion between SpaceX (about 28 launches), ULA (about 19) and Blue Origin (about 7) [A] ([Wikipedia: National Security Space Launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Space_Launch)). China is the only second ecosystem at scale: a Long March 10B first stage was caught in a net at sea on 20 July 2026, and Zhuque-3 made China's first landing on legs on 18-19 August 2026 before a fire toppled it [A] ([ThePrint, Aug 2026](https://theprint.in/feature/china-lands-reusable-rocket-booster-on-ground-for-first-time/3019576/)). Inference (note 03): Chinese reusables are 1-2 years behind Falcon-style reuse and effectively closed to Western compute builders; Blue Origin is the most plausible Western price discipline by 2031-2035 but should be scheduled 2-4 years behind its claims.

#### Failure rates

Falcon 9 had flown 650 times by 12 June 2026 with 647 successes, two failures and one partial failure (about 0.46% lifetime, inference), and 607 of 618 booster landings [A] ([Wikipedia: Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9)), though four upper-stage problems in 19 months led to a grounding in February 2026 [A] ([AIAA, 4 Feb 2026](https://aiaa.org/2026/02/04/spacex-grounds-falcon-9-after-upper-stage-deorbit-issue/)). New Glenn's third flight on 19 April 2026 reused a booster but left its customer's satellite in an unusable orbit; the vehicle was grounded until 22 May [A] ([SatNews, 19 Apr 2026](http://satnews.com/2026/04/19/blue-origin-achieves-first-booster-reuse-but-satellite-enters-off-nominal-orbit/)), and on 28 May 2026 a New Glenn exploded in a static-fire test, wrecking the pad [A] ([Jatan Mehta, Moon Monday #277, 1 Jun 2026](https://jatan.space/moon-monday-issue-277/)). Globally, failure rates were about 6% in 2023, 3% in 2024 (8 of 259) and about 2.4% in 2025 (8 of 329), concentrated in new vehicles [A] ([Seradata/Slingshot, Jan 2025](https://www.slingshot.space/seradata-blog/2024-launch-failure-rate-improves-despite-late-losses); [McDowell, 4 Feb 2026](https://planet4589.org/space/papers/space25.1.4.pdf)). Inference (note 03): about 0.5% loss per launch for a mature vehicle, 5-15% for one with fewer than ten flights, 20-40% for a first flight of a new variant, and a fleet grounding of 2-6 weeks after any failure. Insurance for launches and satellites is covered in sections 2.7 and 2.8.

#### Cislunar transport: a million dollars a kilogram today

NASA plans on about **$1 million per kg delivered to the lunar surface** [A, planning figure]; its Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) task orders, under which companies deliver NASA payloads to the Moon, run from about $47 million (IM-2) to $199.5 million (Griffin/VIPER) [A] ([Wikipedia: CLPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services)). Astrobotic's 2021 list prices were $300,000/kg to lunar orbit, $1.2 million/kg to the surface and $4.5 million/kg on a rover [A] ([Astrobotic Payload User's Guide v5.0](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/astrobotic-lunar-landers-pug.pdf)), and Payload Research calls CLPS margins "razor-thin" [A/C] ([Payload, 21 Feb 2024](https://payloadspace.com/payload-research-the-ultra-low-cost-economics-of-nasas-clps-lunar-program/)). Starship's lunar lander (HLS) holds $2.89 billion plus a $1.15 billion option, can carry 100 t to the surface, and needs a disputed number of refuelling tanker flights per landing (4-8 per Musk in 2021, 16 per GAO in 2023, "high teens" per a NASA official, "10-ish" per a SpaceX vice-president in 2024); its uncrewed demonstration has slipped to 2027 and the first crewed landing to early 2028 [A contracts, C counts, B schedule] ([Wikipedia: Starship HLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_HLS)). Inference (note 03): about 15 launches at the $90 million Starlab price is about $1.35 billion per 100 t landing, about $13,500/kg, so lunar delivery plausibly stays near $1 million/kg until about 2029-2030 and falls to $10,000-50,000/kg once Starship cargo landings are routine in the early-to-mid 2030s.

### 2.4 Lunar resources and ISRU: the physics favours the Moon, the evidence does not yet

Two corrections from note 04 apply throughout this section. First, the ULA lunar-propellant prices circulating in the brief were inverted and attributed to the wrong executive: they were stated on 29 June 2016 by **George Sowers, ULA's vice-president of advanced programs, not Tory Bruno**, and they were **$3,000/kg for propellant in low Earth orbit, $1,000/kg at the Earth-Moon L1 point and $500/kg on the lunar surface** ([Space.com, Jun 2016](https://space.com/33297-satellite-refueling-business-proposal-ula.html)); the brief's "$500/kg in LEO" puts the surface price in the wrong place (notes 04, 07 and 08). Second, Blue Origin's "Blue Alchemist" announcement is dated 10 February 2023, not February 2025 ([Blue Origin, 10 Feb 2023](https://www.blueorigin.com/news/blue-alchemist-powers-our-lunar-future)).

#### Water ice: real, but not yet a reserve

Ice in the polar permanently shadowed regions is real, but its grade, depth and tonnage are unknown at mining scale. The only direct in-place measurement is NASA's LCROSS impact into Cabeus crater in October 2009, which found water at "5.6 ± 2.9% by mass" in the ejecta [A] ([Wikipedia: Lunar water, citing Colaprete et al., Science 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_water)). India's Chandrayaan-1 found hydroxyl bound to soil across the surface [A]; its Mini-SAR radar suggested "an estimated 600 million metric tonnes" of ice in more than 40 north-polar craters, but radar brightness "is not uniquely diagnostic of either roughness or ice", so the estimate is contested [C, methodological limit A] (same source). NASA's SOFIA aircraft found molecular water on the sunlit surface at only about 100-400 parts per million [A], a thermal model puts the total area of permanent cold traps at about 40,000 km² [C], and a 2026 Chandrayaan-2 study finds over 74% of south-polar shadowed regions largely undisturbed by impacts, which favours ice survival [C] (same source). Former ULA executive George Sowers told Congress that water is "billions of metric tons per pole by some estimates" [D] ([Sowers testimony, House Science Committee](https://democrats-science.house.gov/imo/media/doc/Sowers%20Testimony.pdf)). Inference (note 04): at LCROSS's 5.6%, about 18 t of soil yields 1 t of water (12-37 t across the error range), while sunlit-surface water at 100-400 ppm would take 2,500-10,000 t of soil per tonne, so only shadowed-crater ice is a resource.

Every ground-truth mission has failed or slipped. NASA's PRIME-1 drill on IM-2 worked for about 10 hours and returned 6.6 GB of data, but because the lander lay on its side "most of the gases were human-made: remnants of rocket fuel and traces of water from Earth" [A] ([The Driller](https://www.thedriller.com/articles/93689-nasas-lunar-drill-survives-a-crash-landing-and-still-gets-the-job-done)). NASA cancelled its VIPER ice rover (July 2024 per the CLPS record; the NASA page gives no date), then on 19 September 2025 revived it for delivery by a Blue Origin Blue Moon MK1 lander [A, contract] ([NASA Science: VIPER](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viper/); [Wikipedia: CLPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services)). After the 28 May 2026 New Glenn pad explosion, the Blue Moon pathfinder moved to the second half of 2027 and VIPER slips "at least" a year from late 2027 [A event, B schedule] ([Jatan Mehta, Moon Monday #277, 1 Jun 2026](https://jatan.space/moon-monday-issue-277/)). China's Chang'e-7, with an orbiter, lander, rover and a hopping probe meant to drop into shadowed craters near Shackleton, was postponed on 23 August 2026 and is now listed for 2027 [A event] ([AP via Local10, 23 Aug 2026](https://www.local10.com/tech/2026/08/23/china-abruptly-postpones-ambitious-change-7-lunar-mission-launch/); [Supercluster](https://supercluster.com/launches/chang-e-7)); a KeepTrack brief claiming it launched on 24 August is contradicted by AP and is treated as erroneous, and Wikipedia's lunar south pole page gives November 2026, so the new date is unconfirmed. Inference (note 04): no company will hold "proven reserves" before a successful prospecting rover, around 2027-2028 at the earliest, and a realistic 2031 company holds one or two sites' worth of ground-truth data.

#### Extracting water and making propellant

The reference design for a commercial plant is Kornuta et al. (2019), a study by 24+ authors from about 25 organisations: **about $4 billion of capital, 2.8 MW of power (2 MW electrical, 0.8 MW thermal) and 2,450 t of water processed a year to meet 450 t a year of propellant demand, earning $2.4 billion a year**, by heating icy soil under a tent until the ice turns to vapour and then splitting the water by electrolysis [C] ([Kornuta et al., REACH, Mar 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352309318300099)). It is a study, not a project; no such plant has been funded. Kornuta's revenue cannot be read as a delivered price: $2.4 billion divides to about $980 per kg of water or about $5,300 per kg of propellant, and without the full paper neither maps to a price in LEO (inference, notes 04 and 08). Its 4.41 kWh per kg for electrolysis is the thermodynamic minimum; real electrolysers need about 6-7 kWh/kg, and a working rule is about 10 kWh per kg of delivered propellant, or about 1 MW of continuous power per 250-350 t a year (inference, note 04).

Advocates and sceptics disagree on whether it pays. Phil Metzger, a prominent ISRU advocate, finds tent sublimation "an order of magnitude better than the threshold for competitiveness even in low Earth orbit", while strip mining sits near the threshold, assuming at least 5% ice [C] ([Metzger, arXiv 2303.09011, Mar 2023](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2303.09011)). A NASA analysis (Jones et al., 2019) finds lunar propellant "breaks even with propellant delivery from Earth at high propellant demands and mission durations with multi-year ISRU system lifetimes", excluding technology development costs [C] ([NASA NTRS 20200002704](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200002704)), and its strip-mining case came out 97% more expensive than Earth propellant on an SLS-based architecture (as reported by Metzger). A NASA concept study puts thermal extraction for the reference plant at about 800 kW and suggests sorting grains without melting could cut it to under 100 W, an untested idea [C-D] ([NASA NTRS 20230008775, Jun 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230008775)). Water extraction has been tested only on the ground (NASA's OVEN thermal system in 2021, microwave extraction in 2009) [A, ground] ([NASA NTRS search](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/search?q=lunar%20water%20extraction%20permanently%20shadowed%20energy)). Inference (note 04): extraction from icy soil is at TRL 4-5 at best, and integrated propellant production with liquefaction at 25-40 K shadowed-crater temperatures has never been demonstrated anywhere.

#### Oxygen from ordinary soil: the most mature process, still kilograms

Lunar soil is rich in oxygen bound into minerals, and over twenty extraction methods have been proposed, but material ISRU "has not yet been implemented in a space mission" [A] ([Wikipedia: In situ resource utilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_situ_resource_utilization)). On the ground, NASA's carbothermal reactor (which reduces soil with carbon at high temperature) reached TRL 6 in thermal-vacuum tests in 2024, with yields and efficiency that "exceeded the program goals" [A, ground] ([NASA NTRS, Jun 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/search?q=Carbothermal%20Reduction%20Demonstration%20CaRD%20oxygen%20regolith)), and a solar-powered prototype was demonstrated end to end in May 2026 [A, ground] ([NASA NTRS 20260002864](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002864)). Molten regolith electrolysis (MRE, melting soil at about 1,600 °C and passing a current through it) produced **an average 0.07 kg of oxygen per hour over 9 hours from 25 kg of simulant** in Lunar Resources Inc.'s LR-1 reactor in a vacuum chamber at Kennedy Space Center [A, ground] ([NASA NTRS 20260003760, Jul 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260003760)), and its electrodes and linings still degrade [A] ([NASA NTRS search](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/search?q=molten%20regolith%20electrolysis)). The flight precedent is MOXIE on Mars, which made 122 g of oxygen from carbon dioxide over 16 runs, peaking at 12 g/h; it proves flight ISRU hardware can work, not that lunar soil processing is mature [A] ([NASA JPL, 6 Sep 2023](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-oxygen-generating-experiment-moxie-completes-mars-mission/)). Blue Origin's Blue Alchemist (10 February 2023) reports making iron, silicon of ">99.999%" purity, aluminium, oxygen, solar cells and cover glass from simulant by MRE, with no vacuum, yield or energy data [A, self-reported] ([Blue Origin](https://www.blueorigin.com/news/blue-alchemist-powers-our-lunar-future)); NASA awarded Blue Origin $35 million for regolith processing in 2023 [A] ([Wikipedia: Blue Origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Origin)). Inference (note 04): a 1,000 t/yr oxygen plant needs about 1,600 LR-1-equivalents running continuously, a scale-up of three to six orders of magnitude; oxygen is 80-89% of rocket propellant by mass, so it is the more defensible first product, but its value depends on a cislunar transport economy existing.

#### Metals and silicon: lunar mass can be passive, never electronic

Nothing structural, photovoltaic or radiator-grade has been made from real lunar material in lunar conditions, and no one has published production rates [A, negative]. Interlune and Vermeer are developing soil-moving equipment, with a demonstration tool targeted for 2028-2029 on NASA's lunar terrain vehicles [B] ([GeekWire, Jul 2026](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/interlune-vermeer-moon-construction-equipment/)). Inference (note 04): realistic lunar products by 2035 are oxygen, bulk soil for shielding and berms, sintered or cast landing pads, and perhaps pilot-scale iron or aluminium feedstock and glass [C-D]; solar cells from lunar silicon at megawatt scale by 2035 are [D]; and the GPUs, memory, networking and power electronics that make up most of a data centre's value are Earth-only for the whole game horizon. Note 08's in-principle split (inference, [D] as a game parameter) is that radiators, structure, shielding, cell substrates, cover glass and wiring could one day be lunar, about 65-85% of orbital mass (30-50% in a conservative early-2030s case of radiators and shielding only); the 65-70% radiator share behind that figure is attributed to IEEE Spectrum by a secondary source and was not seen in the primary ([Softwareseni, 14 Sep 2026, secondary](https://www.softwareseni.com/what-orbital-data-centres-really-cost-compared-with-terrestrial)).

#### Helium-3: a kilogram market wearing a fusion costume

Helium-3 sits at about 1.4-15 parts per billion in sunlit soil (up to 50 ppb modelled in shadowed regions), so one gram needs "over 150 tonnes of regolith" [C] ([Wikipedia: Helium-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium-3)). Today's supply comes from the decay of tritium in nuclear weapons stockpiles; US demand peaked at about 8 kg a year in 2008 and prices rose from about $100 to as much as $2,000 per litre [A], and deuterium-helium-3 fusion is much harder than the deuterium-tritium fusion every leading programme pursues [C] (same source). The leading company, Interlune, has real but small assets: an $18 million seed round and grants including up to $4.8 million from the Texas Space Commission [A], a SILT excavator designed to ingest 100 t of soil an hour (a $150,000 NASA contract), a target of 10 kg a year from its first harvest "in the 2030s" against prices "up to $20 million per kilogram", and a claim of "over $500 million" in purchase orders and government contracts [A for grants, B for plans, D for the unverified total] ([GeekWire, 4 Feb 2026](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/interlune-excavator-helium-3-moon-construction/)). NASA awarded it a $6.9 million contract on 4 May 2026 for a "Prospect Moon" payload to heat lunar soil and measure helium-3 release on a 2028 CLPS lander [A contract, B mission] ([Interlune, May 2026](https://www.interlune.space/press-release/interlune-awarded-6-9-million-contract-from-nasa-for-lunar-resource-development)). Its "Cold Capture" process extracted 99% pure helium-3 from ordinary terrestrial helium and could make up to 2.5 kg a year from US helium plants, competing with its own lunar product [A demo, B scale] ([Interlune, Jul 2026](https://www.interlune.space/press-release/interlune-produces-pure-helium-3-from-domestic-helium-using-novel-cryogenic-technology)). Buyers are kilogram-scale: the US Department of Energy ordered 3 litres for delivery by April 2029 (billed as the first government purchase of a non-terrestrial resource), and Maybell Quantum intends to buy "thousands of liters annually" in 2029-2035 if lunar production starts by 2029 [A/B] ([SpaceInsider, 8 May 2025](https://spaceinsider.tech/?p=29808)); Bluefors holds a "binding purchase order" per Interlune, but the brief's terms (up to 10,000 L a year for 2028-2037) are **not verified**. Interlune's January 2026 SAFE offering had sold only $0.5 million of $5 million [A] ([GeekWire, 29 Jan 2026](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/interlune-funding-moon-mining-safe/)). Inference (note 04): 10 kg a year means digging at least 1.5 million tonnes of soil and about 25 MW of heat; 10,000 litres is only about 1.3 kg; tenfold more supply would collapse the price; and helium-3 has no bearing on supplying orbital data centres.

#### Getting material off the Moon: the advantage is real but location-dependent

The speed changes are the genuine basis for lunar supply: about 9.3-10 km/s from Earth's surface to LEO, against 1.87 km/s from the lunar surface to low lunar orbit and 2.52 km/s to the Earth-Moon L1 point; but going from LEO to L1 costs 3.77 km/s, and returning to low Earth orbit can be cut by up to 3.2 km/s with aerobraking (dipping into the atmosphere to slow down) [A] ([Wikipedia: Delta-v budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta-v_budget)). Inference (note 04): with hydrogen-oxygen engines, reaching L1 from the Moon burns about 0.8 kg of propellant per kg delivered, against about 7.6 kg per kg from Earth's surface to LEO, and escaping the Moon takes about 12 times less energy per kg. But the advantage is largest at L1, lunar orbit and GEO, and smallest in LEO, where most data-centre concepts sit, because lunar mass bound for LEO must lose 2.5-3 km/s with a heat shield or aerobraking and reusable tugs.

The price points behind lunar business cases are old. Sowers's 2016 figures were willingness-to-pay statements, never contracted, made when Earth launch cost about $2,000/kg or more, for an upper stage (ACES) that was to fly in the early-to-mid 2020s and never did [D] ([Space.com, Jun 2016](https://space.com/33297-satellite-refueling-business-proposal-ula.html)); in later congressional testimony he used $500/kg on the surface and $1,000/kg at L1 [C] ([Sowers testimony](https://democrats-science.house.gov/imo/media/doc/Sowers%20Testimony.pdf)). Metzger's model takes Falcon 9 at about $2,000/kg and its optimistic case reaches "$30/kg to LEO" by year 30, needing about eight Starship launches a day; it states no explicit break-even launch price [C] ([Metzger, arXiv 2303.09011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.09011)). No published study gives a clean break-even Earth launch price (gap). Inference (note 04, large uncertainty): lunar supply plausibly competes in cislunar space when Earth-to-LEO launch is above about $300-1,000/kg, and in LEO only above about $1,000-3,000/kg; a Starship-era price of a few hundred dollars per kg therefore makes the 2016 price points obsolete for LEO customers. The design model's equivalent result (section 2.5) is that lunar passive mass must be delivered, fabricated, below about $600-1,000/kg in 2031-2035 (base case) to beat Earth-built and Earth-launched mass in LEO. Mass drivers (electromagnetic launchers) have no flight or lunar-test heritage, so any use before 2040 is [D] (inference, note 04).

#### Power on the Moon: a few hundred metres of good ridge and a 100 kW reactor

"No points on the Moon receive perpetual light during both the winter and summer" [C]. Two nearby ridge points at Shackleton are lit about 94% of a lunar year together, one point reaches 86%, and the best-lit ridges near the pole are each "much like an island of no more than a few hundred meters across" [C] ([Wikipedia: Peak of eternal light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_of_eternal_light)); Elvis, Milligan and Krolikowski put the total at about one square kilometre and warn that a facility there would need "protective exclusion and de facto appropriation" [C] ([arXiv 1608.01989, Space Policy 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.01989)). Modelled points 2-10 m above the Connecting Ridge are lit 92-96% of the time with darkness of only 3-5 days at most [C] ([Wikipedia: Lunar south pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_south_pole)). NASA's August 2025 directive seeks a fission reactor of at least 100 kW of electricity, under 15 t, launching in the first quarter of fiscal 2030 [B] ([NEI Magazine, Aug 2025](https://www.neimagazine.com/news/nasa-fast-tracks-lunar-reactor-plan/); [Military & Aerospace Electronics, Aug 2025](https://militaryaerospace.com/home/article/55310198/nasa-seeks-industry-input-on-lunar-and-mars-fission-power-system-for-2030-launch)); its 2022 design studies were three $5 million contracts for a 40 kW, under-6 t system, and in March 2026 NASA announced "Lunar Reactor-1" [A] ([Wikipedia: Fission Surface Power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_Surface_Power)). Whether the 2025 procurement produced awards could not be confirmed. Russia and China agreed in May 2025 to build a lunar nuclear power station for their joint base by 2035 [B] ([ProPakistani, 20 May 2025, secondary](https://propakistani.pk/2025/05/20/china-and-russia-to-build-a-nuclear-power-station-on-the-moon/amp/)). Inference (note 04): one 100 kW reactor is about 5% of the reference plant's electricity, so a plant needs about 20 such reactors (about 300 t landed) or megawatt-scale solar on contested ridges with storage through dark spells of several days; megawatt-scale lunar power before 2035 is [D].

#### Players and programme status

Artemis II flew four astronauts around the Moon from 1 to 10 April 2026 (NASA gives splashdown at 5:07 p.m. PDT on 10 April, which is 11 April in UTC, explaining Wikipedia's "11 April") [A] ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-detail/artemis-ii-splashdown-2/); [Wikipedia: Artemis II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_II)). In February 2026 NASA turned Artemis III into a crewed docking test with prototype landers in low Earth orbit, targeted for 2027 ("mid-2027" per Flying in April, "no earlier than late 2027" per later reporting), and moved the first landing to Artemis IV in early 2028, which Administrator Isaacman calls "very ambitious"; SpaceX's lander contract is being reopened to competition [B] ([CNN via KVIA, 27 Feb 2026](https://kvia.com/cnn-other/2026/02/27/nasa-abruptly-adds-a-new-step-to-its-path-to-moon-landing-as-a-race-with-china-looms/); [Wikipedia: Artemis III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_III); [Flying, 11 Apr 2026](https://www.flyingmag.com/next-for-nasa-artemis-moon-mission-program/); [The Register, 1 May 2026](https://www.theregister.com/2026/05/01/artemis_iii_aims_for_late/)). China aims to land astronauts "before 2030"; its Lanyue lander passed a landing-and-takeoff test in August 2025 and its Long March 10 rocket flew a low-altitude demonstration in February 2026 [B] ([Wikipedia: Lanyue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanyue); [CGTN, 27 Feb 2026](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-02-27/China-plans-two-crewed-space-missions-for-2026-eyes-2030-moon-landing-1L6i9f4Z9le/share_amp.html)). The China-Russia International Lunar Research Station (ILRS) plans construction in 2026-2035 and a basic station by 2035, with 13 member states per Wikipedia or "17 countries and international organisations" per China's space agency (April 2025), and Chang'e-8 in 2028 (Wikipedia) or 2029 (a later secondary source) [B] ([Wikipedia: ILRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Lunar_Research_Station); [SpaceOdysseyHub, 24 Sep 2026](https://spaceodysseyhub.com/programs/international-lunar-research-station-ilrs)).

Commercial landers have a poor record. Astrobotic's Peregrine (January 2024) never attempted a landing; Intuitive Machines' IM-1 (February 2024) landed and tipped over; Firefly's Blue Ghost M1 (landed 2 March 2025) was the "first fully successful landing of the CLPS Program"; IM-2 (March 2025) tipped over; and ispace's second lander crashed on 5 June 2025 [A] ([Wikipedia: CLPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services); [Wikipedia: Hakuto-R Mission 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakuto-R_Mission_2)). Sources classify IM-2 as a "failure" (CLPS page) or a "partial" landing on its side (Moon landing page). Next up are Astrobotic's Griffin-1 (November 2026) and IM-3, Blue Ghost M2 and the Blue Moon pathfinder (early 2027, the last now the second half of 2027) [B] ([Wikipedia: CLPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services)). On 26 May 2026 NASA selected Astrolab, with Interlune on its team, for the Artemis lunar terrain vehicle, with a task order of about $219 million under a $4.6 billion contract vehicle [A] ([Interlune, May 2026](https://www.interlune.space/press-release/nasa-selects-astrolab-to-provide-lunar-rover-for-artemis-astronauts-return-to-the-moon)); Wikipedia and Jatan Mehta instead describe two finalists (Astrolab and Lunar Outpost), a conflict not resolved ([Wikipedia: Lunar Terrain Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Terrain_Vehicle)). The only lunar resource sales ever contracted are NASA's 2020 regolith purchases, **$25,001 in total** (Lunar Outpost $1, ispace Japan and Europe $5,000 each, Masten $15,000), legal-precedent instruments rather than a market [A] ([NASA, 3 Dec 2020](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-companies-to-collect-lunar-resources-for-artemis-demonstrations/)). Resource start-ups such as Starpath Robotics, Karman+ and Magna Petra were not researched.

#### Law in brief (detail in 2.6)

The Outer Space Treaty bars any claim of sovereignty over a mine site, but the US (2015), Luxembourg (2017), the UAE (2019) and Japan (2021) let their companies own what they extract; Japan issued its first licence to ispace on 8 November 2022 [A]. Whether extraction is lawful "use" or forbidden "appropriation" is unsettled internationally, the UN working group will produce only non-binding principles by 2027, and the Artemis Accords' self-declared "safety zones" around operations at the same few south-polar ridges are the likeliest flashpoint, a point US officials have made explicitly about reactors and "keep-out zones" (section 2.6).

#### Lunar risks

All lunar soft-landing attempts from 2019 to 2025 succeeded fully only 5 times in 13 (about 38%), with 3 partial and 5 failures; China's state programme was 3 for 3, and commercial landers 1 clean success in 7 missions [A, counts by note 06] ([Wikipedia: Moon landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_landing)). Apollo's dust problems fell into nine categories, from clogged mechanisms and abrasion to failed seals and heat-rejection loss, and "the severity of the dust problems were consistently underestimated by ground tests" [A] ([Gaier, NASA GRC, 2005](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20050160460)). Japan's SLIM, not designed to survive the lunar night at about -120 °C, nonetheless woke up for four lunar days [A] ([Wikipedia: SLIM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_Lander_for_Investigating_Moon)), so night survival is uncertain rather than impossible. Inference (notes 04 and 06): use a per-landing full-success probability of about 30-60% for commercial landers in 2026-2030, improving with each flight of the same design; assume crewed-demand dates slip 2-4 years; and treat dust and night as a continuing reliability tax plus a "rival claims the ridge" event, since Chang'e-7 and Blue Origin's pathfinder both target the Shackleton rim.

#### What this dossier adds about the model (inference, this dossier)

The "dearer even at $0/kg" result rests on the orbital penalties applied to the GPU bill, not on launch. In the 2035 base case orbit's GPU line alone is $11.05M against the ground's $9.21M per MW per year, a premium of $1.84M that nearly equals everything orbit avoids on the ground (facility $1.67M, energy $1.01M and other running costs $0.30M, together $2.98M) before orbit pays for its satellite hardware ($2.18M) and launch ($1.09M). Re-running the same script with only GPU-life and spares changed shows how sensitive this is: the 2033 base case with a 7-year life and 10% spares gives $12.35M (1.01x ground); adding a 10.3% cost of capital gives $11.7M (0.96x); and a vertically integrated launcher ($60/kg launch, $400/kg hardware) with a 7-year life gives $10.75M (0.88x). With launch at $0/kg and everything else at base, orbit is 1.32x (2031), 1.20x (2033) and 1.10x (2035). **Whether orbit can ever undercut the ground therefore hinges on how long GPUs survive and stay useful in orbit and how many spares are needed, numbers for which there is no flight data** (Starcloud-1's results are unpublished). Four limits of the model matter for the content pack: it assumes identical GPU prices (a premium for space-rated or hotter-running chips is unknown); orbital ground stations and links are a designed $0.25M per MW per year; it counts no revenue, so the scarcity rent behind finding 2 sits outside the per-MW cost; and it prices no debris, regulatory or insurance-market costs beyond a self-insured launch loss.

The model also disagrees with one credible source. Forethought's conditional model has orbit about 5-7% cheaper per GW than a ground site (microgrid or grid-connected) at $100/kg launch, whereas the design model does not reach parity at any launch price in 2031-2033. The likely reason is that the design model loads the GPU capex with orbital penalties (20% spares, a 5-year life, a higher cost of capital and launch loss), while Forethought applies its ~38% failure penalty to non-compute hardware; Forethought's full assumptions were not visible, so the difference cannot be fully reconciled (gap).

### 2.6 Law, regulation and geopolitics: permissive at home, contested on the Moon

#### The treaties: states answer for their companies

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty (OST) is the binding base layer. Outer space "is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any other means" (Article II); states bear international responsibility for national space activities "whether conducted by government or private entities" (Article VI), are liable for damage their space objects cause (Article VII), and must avoid harmful contamination (Article IX) [A] ([UNOOSA, Outer Space Treaty](https://www.unoosa.org/oosa/en/ourwork/spacelaw/treaties/introouterspacetreaty.html)). Article VIII gives "jurisdiction and control over a space object to its state of registry", and the ISS, where each partner keeps jurisdiction over its own modules, is the template for platforms shared between countries [A] ([DC Bar Washington Lawyer, Sep/Oct 2026](https://dcbar.org/news-events/publications/washington-lawyer-articles/will-in-space-data-centers-take-privacy-laws-beyon)). Under the 1972 Liability Convention, the "launching State" (the state that launches, procures the launch, or whose territory is used) is liable without proof of fault for damage on Earth or to aircraft, but **damage in space, such as one satellite hitting another, requires proof of fault** [A] ([UNOOSA, Liability Convention](https://www.unoosa.org/oosa/en/ourwork/spacelaw/treaties/introliability-convention.html)). The 1979 Moon Agreement, with its "common heritage of mankind" language, has 17 parties and no major launching power, and Saudi Arabia's withdrawal took effect on 5 January 2024 [A] ([UN Treaty Collection](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/MTDSG/Volume%20II/Chapter%20XXIV/XXIV-2.en.xml)); a 2023 law-firm piece still listing Saudi Arabia as bound is outdated ([IBA, 18 Jan 2023](https://www.ibanet.org/disputes-over-space-mining-on-the-horizon)). Inference (note 05): a US company launching from New Zealand on a US rocket could make both countries "launching States", a collision between rival data-centre satellites is a fault dispute possibly between states rather than automatic compensation, and the Moon Agreement is a non-factor for US, EU and Japanese players.

#### Space resources: four countries say you can own what you dig

The US (2015) entitles its citizens to "possess, own, transport, use, and sell" space resources they obtain, "in accordance with applicable law, including the international obligations of the United States" [A] ([51 U.S.C. §51303](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/51/51303)). Luxembourg's 2017 law states "Space resources are capable of being owned" but requires a written ministerial licence, a Luxembourg-registered company with its central administration there, financial cover, two qualified managers, continuous supervision and an annual audit [A] ([Luxembourg Space Agency](https://space-agency.public.lu/en/agency/legal-framework/law_space_resources_english_translation.html)). Japan's 2021 Space Resources Act grants ownership to permit holders, and ispace received the first licence on 8 November 2022 for a planned sale of soil to NASA [A] ([Reddie & Grose, 15 Aug 2024](https://www.reddie.co.uk/2024/08/15/japanese-national-space-law-leading-the-way/)); the UAE (2019) also legislates for resource activities [A] ([Opinio Juris, 8 Nov 2022](https://opiniojuris.org/2022/11/08/can-national-laws-on-space-resources-serve-as-evidence-of-customary-international-law/)). None claims the ground itself, and whether extraction is permitted "use" or forbidden "appropriation" is unsettled; one scholar doubts national laws can become customary international law "without Russia or China supporting [the US] reading" [C] (same source), and Holland & Knight says "it remains to be seen whether other states will recognise a title to space resources based only on domestic law" [C] ([IBA](https://www.ibanet.org/disputes-over-space-mining-on-the-horizon)). The UN COPUOS working group on space resources aims to finalise "initial recommended principles" by 2027 for possible General Assembly adoption, which would be non-binding [B] ([UNOOSA working group](https://www.unoosa.org/oosa/en/ourwork/copuos/lsc/space-resources/index.html)); its unofficial July 2025 draft puts non-appropriation and state supervision in an agreed part, and the US-style clause that extraction "does not inherently constitute national appropriation", safety zones and benefit-sharing in a "no consensus" part [C] ([UNOOSA draft, 7 Jul 2025](https://www.unoosa.org/documents/pdf/copuos/lsc/space-resources/Updated_Initial_draft_set_of_recommended_principles_WG_SRA.pdf)). Iran's April 2026 statement, that the draft "primarily addresses interactions among capable spacefaring nations", shows the developing-world push for benefit-sharing [A] ([Iran mission, 15 Apr 2026](https://viennaun.mfa.gov.ir/files/viennaunen/newsattachment/202604151634378928543716.pdf)). Inference (note 05): lunar ore has clean title only in markets that recognise it, a binding treaty before 2035 is [D], and choosing a licensing jurisdiction is a real strategic lever.

#### Artemis Accords against ILRS: two blocs, one south pole

**76 countries had signed the non-binding Artemis Accords by late September 2026**, San Marino being the 76th on 25 September [A] ([Wikipedia: Artemis Accords, accessed 5 Oct 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords); [NASA release via SpaceLaunchSchedule](https://www.spacelaunchschedule.com/space-news/nasa-welcomes-san-marino-signing-the-artemis-accords/)); recent signers include Türkiye on 31 August 2026 ([Türkiye MFA, 1 Sep 2026](https://www.mfa.gov.tr/no_-170_-ulkemizin-artemis-mutabakati-ni-imzalamasi-hk.en.mfa)). Section 10 says extraction "does not inherently constitute national appropriation", and Section 11 lets signatories announce "safety zones" whose "size and scope should be based on the nature and environment of the operations involved and determined in a reasonable manner" [A] ([Wikipedia: Artemis Accords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords)). The China-Russia ILRS counted "17 countries and international organisations and more than 50 international research institutions" in April 2025 and plans a basic south-polar station by 2035 [B] ([SpaceOdysseyHub, 24 Sep 2026](https://spaceodysseyhub.com/programs/international-lunar-research-station-ilrs)); Russia and China agreed in May 2025 to build a lunar nuclear power station for it by 2035 [B] ([ProPakistani, 20 May 2025](https://propakistani.pk/2025/05/20/china-and-russia-to-build-a-nuclear-power-station-on-the-moon/amp/)). Announcing the US reactor push on 5 August 2025, the then-acting NASA head Sean Duffy warned that the first country with a lunar reactor could "declare a keep-out zone which would significantly inhibit the United States" [B] ([UPI, 5 Aug 2025](https://www.upi.com/Science_News/2025/08/05/science-sean-duffy-nuclear-reactor-moon/2301754399672/)). Shackleton, Haworth and Nobile craters appear on both NASA's and China's candidate landing lists, the 2011 Wolf Amendment bars NASA from bilateral work with Chinese space bodies without approval, and no formal deconfliction mechanism exists [C, 2022] ([JHU Space Security, 18 Sep 2022](https://spacesecurity.wse.jhu.edu/?p=1728)). The blocs are not exclusive: Serbia signed the Accords on 16 July 2026 and is a named ILRS partner. Inference (note 05): self-declared safety zones around reactors and mines on the same few ridges are the real flashpoint, most likely in 2028-2032; no physical incident has happened yet.

#### Licensing a giant constellation: the US is lowering the bar

A US compute constellation needs an FCC space-station licence, an international frequency filing through the US administration at the ITU (International Telecommunication Union), an FAA licence for each launch, a NOAA licence if it images Earth, and possibly a new "novel activity" certification (note 05). The FCC adopted a new Part 100 licensing regime on 22 July 2026, replacing its old satellite rules with a "licensing assembly line" and requiring operators to share space-situational-awareness data (tracking data on where their satellites are) [A] ([FCC, 22 Jul 2026](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-423287A1.txt)). Outside a processing round, a system must launch one satellite within 7 years, then deploy 10% within 9 years, 50% within 12 and 100% within 14; the surety bond becomes optional [A] ([DLA Piper, Jul 2026](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/07/fcc-overhauls-space-and-satellite-licensing-process-key-changes)). The ITU's own rule (Resolution 35) requires 10% within 2 years after the bringing-into-use deadline, 50% within 5 and all within 7, to stop "warehousing" of spectrum [A] ([ITU](https://itu.int/en/mediacentre/backgrounders/Pages/Non-geostationary-satellite-systems.aspx)). An August 2025 executive order directed streamlined launch licensing and an authorisation process for novel activities [A] ([EO 14335, GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/DCPD-202500847/html/DCPD-202500847.htm)), and on 24 March 2026 the Office of Space Commerce proposed a voluntary, "light-touch" certification naming "orbital computing" and "lunar operations", with a 120-day review and a presumption of approval [B, proposal] ([DLA Piper, 14 Apr 2026](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2026/04/osc-proposes-voluntary-certification-framework)). No FCC decision on the SpaceX or Orbital applications was found. The EU Space Act, proposed in June 2025, would require operators serving EU customers (including non-EU ones, via an EU legal representative) to meet safety, cybersecurity and environmental rules; the Commission proposed applying it from 1 January 2030, Council and Parliament favour 36 months after adoption, and adoption is "unlikely before late 2027" [B, C for timing] ([Hogan Lovells, 15 May 2026](https://hlc.com/en/publications/the-eu-space-act-proposal-in-the-legislative-process-cybersecurity-market-access-and-implications)); the Council was still negotiating core issues in May 2026, so reports that three-way talks are "underway" are unconfirmed ([US Office of Space Commerce, 12 May 2026](https://space.commerce.gov/?p=14622)). Inference (note 05): the US will be a permissive home for 2031-2035 constellations, the EU Space Act lands around 2030-2031 as a mid-act compliance shock, and the million-satellite filings are options, not plans.

#### Export controls: orbit is not a loophole

The US rescinded the Biden-era "AI Diffusion" rule on 13 May 2025, but the core controls on advanced AI chips (export classifications 3A090 and 4A090) "remain fully in place", with guidance targeting diversion through cloud providers [A] ([Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/bis-rescinds-ai-diffusion-rule-and-issues-new-guidance)). On 15 January 2026 sales of NVIDIA H200 and AMD MI325X chips to China moved to case-by-case review under conditions, a 25% tariff and a cap at 50% of US shipment volumes [A, secondary; Federal Register text not verified]; the House passed the Remote Access Security Act, aimed at cloud GPU rental loopholes, 369-22 on 12 January 2026 [B; Senate status unknown] ([Introl](https://introl.com/blog/bis-export-policy-h200-mi325x-china-case-by-case-2026)). US export rules define export as shipment out of the United States "in any manner" and include transferring "registration, control, or ownership" of a controlled spacecraft to a national of the restricted country group that includes China; launch into space is not itself listed [A] ([15 CFR 734.13](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-15/subtitle-B/chapter-VII/subchapter-C/part-734/section-734.13); [22 CFR 120.50](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-22/chapter-I/subchapter-M/part-120/subpart-C/section-120.50)). Inference (note 05): launching US chips from a foreign spaceport is a physical export needing the usual licence analysis, selling the satellite to a Chinese buyer faces a presumption of denial, renting orbital compute to Chinese customers could become controlled if the remote-access bill passes, and the H200 policy has flipped twice in about 18 months, so export licences are a swingy political variable.

#### Data sovereignty: whose law runs on an orbiting server

The state of registry's law governs the satellite and, by extension, data processed on board. The EU's GDPR privacy law can still reach a space data centre that has an EU establishment, targets or monitors EU residents, or is registered by an EU state, although enforcement depends on assets and ground stations on Earth, and the EU data-protection board itself concedes that asserting such reach is "questionable at best" under international law [C] ([DC Bar Washington Lawyer, Sep/Oct 2026](https://dcbar.org/news-events/publications/washington-lawyer-articles/will-in-space-data-centers-take-privacy-laws-beyon)). Lonestar pitches that a US-registered craft "can carry a black box inside it that's under British law, or any other country's" [C/D, company claim] ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/data-center-on-the-moon)); inference (note 05): that has no treaty basis without the customer state registering the module or a bilateral agreement, so "sovereign cloud in orbit" is a contract and diplomacy product, and sovereign customers pay a premium only if the registry state suits them.

#### Debris rules: one binding US rule, everything else voluntary or underfunded

The FCC adopted a "5-Year Rule for Deorbiting Satellites" in 2022 [A] ([FCC](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-adopts-new-5-year-rule-deorbiting-satellites)); its exact terms and treatment under Part 100 were not confirmed. ESA's Zero Debris Charter, signed by 12 states in May 2024, targets a 99% success rate for removing satellites at end of life by 2030 [A] ([Access Partnership](https://accesspartnership.com/access-alert-12-countries-sign-zero-debris-charter-pivot-to-space-sustainability)); inference (note 05): even 99% across a 100,000-1,000,000-satellite constellation leaves 1,000-10,000 dead satellites. The civil US traffic-coordination system TraCSS tracks more than 11,000 satellites for 62 pilot users, but the FY2027 budget request cut the Office of Space Commerce by about 80% to $11 million without explicitly funding it [A] ([FedScoop, 17 Jul 2026](https://fedscoop.com/space-commerce-official-tracss-continues-despite-budget-uncertainty/)). Against SpaceX's application, Amazon petitioned to deny it, calling it "at best...an exercise in publicity and messaging" and "at worst, an attempt to stake a priority claim over a vast swath of orbital resources", the American Astronomical Society called it "an unprecedented threat to the visual integrity of the night sky", and FCC Chair Brendan Carr dismissed Amazon's petition [A, filings] ([Via Satellite, 11 Mar 2026](https://www.satellitetoday.com/connectivity/2026/03/11/amazons-petition-to-deny-spacex-orbital-data-constellation-draws-criticism-from-brendan-carr)). A 2026 preprint models up to 79,000-98,000 SpaceX data-centre satellites brighter than magnitude 5 (visible to the naked eye) at 30°N in winter twilight, "approximately 100 times more visible satellites" than visible stars [C, preprint] ([Boley, Lawler and Rein, arXiv 2608.02757](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02757)). No regulator caps constellation size; the friction comes through filings, objections and milestones (inference, note 05).

#### Military and sovereign interest: dual use from day one

Orbital compute is inherently dual-use. China's Three-Body satellites are a Zhejiang Lab programme with laser links between six satellites and a plan for 1,000+ [A/B] ([eHangzhou/Xinhua, 14 Feb 2026](https://www.ehangzhou.gov.cn/2026-02/14/c_296817.htm)), and the US Golden Dome missile-defence plan (announced 20 May 2025) envisages 400-1,000 tracking satellites and about 200 armed satellites [B] ([NBC Montana/Sinclair, 20 May 2025](https://nbcmontana.com/news/nation-world/president-donald-trump-defense-secretary-pete-hegseth-pentagon-to-unveil-golden-dome-missile-defense-plan-space-x-weapons-space-force-attack-satellites)); its cost is $175 billion per the White House, raised to $185 billion in March 2026, against Congressional Budget Office estimates of $161-542 billion over 20 years for space-based parts [A/B, C] ([Wikipedia: Golden Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Dome_%28missile_defense_system%29)). Destructive anti-satellite (ASAT) tests have poisoned orbits for decades: China's 2007 test made over 3,500 trackable fragments, more than 2,500 still in orbit 18 years later, and Russia's 2021 test made more than 1,800 and forced an ISS manoeuvre [A] ([Secure World Foundation, 2025](https://swfound.org/media/208110/04_focus-issue-continuing-the-destructive-da-asat-missile-test.pdf)). Thirty-eight countries had pledged not to conduct such tests by January 2025, but China and Russia voted against the 2022 UN resolution calling for that (155-9-9) [A] ([SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/u-n-approves-resolution-not-to-conduct-destructive-asat-tests)), and Russia vetoed a 2024 Security Council resolution reaffirming the ban on nuclear weapons in orbit [A per note 05; note 06 could not open the primary] ([UN press release SC/15678](https://press.un.org/en/2024/sc15678.doc.htm)). Inference (note 05): a US operator would likely win defence demand but face launch-priority conflicts, pressure to refuse Chinese customers and a higher targeting profile, while Chinese competitors are policy instruments rather than purely commercial rivals; the political-risk ladder runs from export-licence flips through regulatory objections, lunar safety-zone disputes and government requisition to jamming and, rarely, destructive or nuclear attack.

### 2.7 The risks that make it high-stakes

The risks below differ from ordinary data-centre risks in one way: orbital assets cannot be repaired, moved or resold to another site, and most of their value is uninsurable.

#### Debris and collision cascades

ESA's Space Environment Report 2026 (14 September 2026) says that "Even if no new satellites were launched, the debris population would continue to increase because fragmentation events generate debris faster than they can naturally reenter" [C], and its environment "health index" (1 = sustainable) went "from about 4 to 50 in just one year" [C] ([ESA, 14 Sep 2026](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2026)). Inference (note 06): that jump coincided with ESA cutting its projection horizon from 200 to 100 years, so the direction is solid but the twelvefold factor should not be used as a physical multiplier. About 9.8 accidental fragmentations happen each year on average, and active satellites crowd 400-600 km, with 500, 550, 700 and 800 km heavily populated [A] ([ESA report, May 2026](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/publications/Space_Environment_Report_I10R0_20260501.pdf)). Starlink made 207,152 collision-avoidance manoeuvres between December 2025 and May 2026, against about 50,000 in all of 2024, with a fleet that grew from about 6,000 to more than 10,000 [A] ([Space.com, 15 Jul 2026](https://space.com/space-exploration/satellites/every-spacex-starlink-satellite-has-to-dodge-a-collision-almost-weekly-and-experts-fear-the-worst)); part of that rise reflects Starlink's very cautious trigger of 3 in 10 million collision probability (inference, note 06). The "CRASH clock", the expected time to a collision if all satellites stopped manoeuvring, fell from 121 days in 2018 to 2.8 days in 2025 [C, preprint] ([Wikipedia: Kessler syndrome, citing Thiele et al.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kessler_syndrome)), and one economic model puts the aggregate threshold for avoiding a runaway cascade (Kessler syndrome) at 72,000 satellites, not split by altitude [C] (same source). Altitude decides how long damage lasts: debris from Russia's 2021 test at about 480 km was almost gone by 2026 (4 of 1,806 catalogued pieces left), while China's 2007 test at 865 km still had 2,319 tracked pieces in mid-2026 [A, secondary] ([KeepTrack, 2026](https://keeptrack.space/top-10/10-anti-satellite-tests)). A Chinese ZhuQue-2E upper stage broke up on 9 June 2026; seven fragments were catalogued while tracking firm LeoLabs estimated 100-150 pieces, showing that catalogues undercount [A, secondary] ([NewSpace Economy, 29 Sep 2026](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/09/29/what-does-nasas-september-2026-orbital-debris-report-reveal-about-the-future-of-crowded-orbits/)). Peter Plavchan (George Mason University) calls SpaceX's million-satellite filing a "first-mover territorial claim strategy in lieu of off-world space regulations" [C/D] ([Broadband Breakfast, Jan 2026](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/spacex-seeks-fcc-approval-for-one-million-satellites-in-space/)). Inference (note 06): "closing an orbit" is economic rather than literal, through more manoeuvres, propellant, downtime and regulatory friction; a 500-600 km data centre sits in the most crowded band but cleans itself relatively fast, while 800-1,000 km shells stay dirty for decades.

#### Solar storms

Solar Cycle 25 peaked in October 2024 at a smoothed sunspot number of 161, well above the 105-125 forecast, and is expected to end around 2030 [A] ([SIDC/STCE](https://www.sidc.be/article/solar-cycle-25-reached-its-maximum-october-2024); [Wikipedia: Solar cycle 25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cycle_25)). Storms have already destroyed satellites at low altitude: up to 40 of 49 freshly launched Starlinks were lost after a minor storm in February 2022 raised drag by up to 50% [A] ([Space.com, Feb 2022](https://www.space.com/spacex-starlink-satellites-lost-geomagnetic-storm)), and around the May 2024 "Gannon" storm, which raised air density at 400 km up to six times and was underpredicted by forecasts, 12 Starlinks at 300-400 km re-entered while those above 500 km were unaffected [A] ([Parker & Linares, arXiv 2406.08617](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08617v2); [Ashruf et al., arXiv 2410.16254](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16254v2)). A Carrington-class storm (the 1859 benchmark for an extreme solar storm) has a 3.0-10.3% chance per decade, with wide confidence bands [C] ([Riley & Love, USGS, 2017](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/extreme-geomagnetic-storms-probabilistic-forecasts-and-their-uncertainties)); one such outburst missed Earth by about nine days of orbital position in July 2012 [A], and Lloyd's estimated a US cost of $0.6-2.6 trillion, mostly through the power grid [C] ([Wikipedia: Carrington Event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrington_Event)). Inference (note 06): that is about 1.5-5% over 2031-2035; drag kills satellites below about 400 km, while a 500+ km data centre is mainly exposed to radiation hits on electronics and to fleet-wide manoeuvre outages; Cycle 26 would peak around 2034-2036 if cycle lengths are typical (no published forecast found).

#### Launch failure, attack and conflict

Launch failure rates were covered in 2.3: about 2.4-6% a year globally, concentrated in new vehicles. There have been four destructive direct-ascent ASAT tests (China 2007, US 2008, India 2019, Russia 2021) [A, secondary] ([KeepTrack](https://keeptrack.space/top-10/10-anti-satellite-tests)). The White House confirmed in February 2024 that Russia was developing an ASAT capability it said would violate the Outer Space Treaty [A] ([Wikipedia: Kosmos 2553](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosmos_2553)), and the 1962 Starfish Prime high-altitude nuclear test "damaged or destroyed roughly one third of all satellites in low Earth orbit at the time" [A] ([War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/getting-serious-about-the-threat-of-high-altitude-nuclear-detonations/)). Electronic warfare and cyber attacks are the only counterspace tools actually used in war, from the 2022 hack of Viasat's KA-SAT network to pervasive GNSS jamming [A/C, think tank] ([SpaceQ on SWF Global Counterspace 2026, 17 Apr 2026](https://spaceq.ca/swf-2026-counterspace-report-highlights-electronic-warfare-threat-and-canadian-defence-implications/)), and Russia has tried to jam Starlink, which experts call "difficult" against the space segment [A, journalistic] ([ModernGhana, 3 Jul 2026](https://www.modernghana.com/news/1507548/russia-faces-challenges-trying-to-jam-starlink.html)). Inference (note 06): destructive ASAT tests ran at about one every four years historically (none since 2021), jamming or cyber interference is near-certain in any major-power conflict, and a nuclear detonation in low orbit is very unlikely but would cripple unhardened fleets over weeks to months.

#### Insurance: a small market that cannot cover a data centre

Space insurance collected about $580 million in premiums in 2024 [A] ([Seradata/Slingshot, 23 Jul 2025](https://www.slingshot.space/seradata-blog/space-insurance-promising-start-to-the-year-marred-only-by-methanesat-claim)), lost more than $1 billion in 2023 (claims about double premiums) and saw insurers including Hiscox, Canopius and Munich Re syndicates leave the class [A] ([Gallagher Q4 2024, 9 Jan 2025](https://specialty.ajg.com/plane-talking/space-market-update-q4-2024)). 2025 was heading for about $500 million of profit for insurers ([Gallagher Q4 2025, 8 Jan 2026](https://specialty.ajg.com/plane-talking/space-market-update-q4-2025)) until the SpainSat NG-2 total loss, "insured for over USD400m", moved the year's loss ratio "from circa 15% to circa 75%"; competitive capacity per risk is "around the USD300m mark" and buyers show a "lack of buyer appetite for insuring satellite constellations" [A/C] ([Gallagher Q1 2026, 10 Apr 2026](https://specialty.ajg.com/plane-talking/space-insurance-market-update-q1-2026)). Only about 300 of more than 10,000 satellites carry insurance, and low-orbit constellations are "largely uninsured or self-insured" [A] ([Canadian Underwriter, 16 Sep 2025](https://canadianunderwriter.ca/2025/09/16/space-jam-why-low-orbital-claims-are-bringing-insurers-back-to-earth/)); a secondary source says SpaceX buys no in-orbit cover for Starlink [C, not verified] ([NewSpace Economy, 29 Mar 2026](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/03/29/the-orbital-insurance-market-how-underwriters-are-pricing-constellation-scale-risk/)). Sources conflict on several figures: 2024 claims were about $211 million (Seradata) or part of a $1.5 billion 2023/24 total (Gallagher), on different bases; 2025 premium was "about $260 million a year" in mid-2025 (NewSpace Economy) whereas Gallagher's loss-ratio arithmetic implies about $650-700 million (prefer Gallagher); and the ViaSat-3 F1 claim is given as about $421 million (Canadian Underwriter) or $770 million ([Taylor Wessing, 2024](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/interface/2024/the-space-race/launch-plus-1-satellite-insurance)). A "$4.43 billion" space-insurance market figure is market-research hype inconsistent with real premiums [D]. Inference (notes 03, 06): a $1 billion loss on one orbital-compute launch would exceed a year's global premium and harden the market for 1-2 years, so builders self-insure, spread value across launches and treat in-orbit losses as hits to equity.

#### Stranded assets and an AI demand bust

The cleanest precedents are the telecom bust and Iridium. After 1996, telecom firms invested "more than $500 billion, mostly financed with debt", the industry owed about "a trillion dollars", and bond investors recovered "just over 20%" [A] ([Wikipedia: Telecoms crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecoms_crash)); the original Iridium filed for bankruptcy nine months after starting service and its system was sold for $25 million, erasing more than $4 billion of debt, before the relaunched company passed a million subscribers [A] ([Wikipedia: Iridium Satellite LLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridium_Satellite_LLC)). On AI demand, Sequoia's David Cahn estimated in June 2024 that about $600 billion a year of AI revenue was needed to justify the spending [C] ([Sequoia, 20 Jun 2024](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/ais-600b-question/)); Bain found that about 200 GW of AI compute by 2030 needs "about $2 trillion in new revenue", leaving an $800 billion shortfall [C] ([PYMNTS on Bain, Sep 2025](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2025/ai-companies-face-800-billion-dollar-funding-shortfall-bain)); and when DeepSeek's efficient model appeared, NVIDIA lost $600 billion of market value on 27 January 2025, the largest one-day drop for any US company [A] ([Wikipedia: DeepSeek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeepSeek)). Inference (note 06): these gaps measure required revenue, not the probability of a crash, which no credible source quantifies; a bust would hit orbit harder than the ground, because orbital assets cannot be repurposed and deorbit within about five years, and the Iridium pattern (bankruptcy, then a buyer profiting from assets bought for about 1% of cost) is a natural "vulture buyer" event.

#### Ground-side breakthroughs that would undercut orbit

The International Energy Agency's base case has data-centre electricity rising from 415 TWh in 2024 to about 945 TWh in 2030 and about 1,200 TWh in 2035, within a 700-1,700 TWh range; its high-efficiency case is 20% lower [C] ([IEA Energy and AI, Apr 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary)). Firm clean power arrives mostly in the 2030s: Commonwealth Fusion's ARC plant (Google has a 200 MW agreement) is planned for "the early 2030s" [B] ([Wikipedia: Commonwealth Fusion Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Fusion_Systems)); Helion's 2028 Microsoft deal draws scepticism after it missed its own 2024 target [B/C] ([Wikipedia: Helion Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helion_Energy)); NuScale's US flagship was cancelled in 2023 after costs rose from $3.6 billion to $9.3 billion, and its first Romanian module is planned for July 2033 [A/B] ([Wikipedia: NuScale Power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NuScale_Power)); Kairos promises Google its first small reactor by 2030 [B] ([Google, 14 Oct 2024](https://blog.google/outreach-initiatives/sustainability/google-kairos-power-nuclear-energy-agreement/)); TerraPower began building its 345 MW Natrium plant in April 2026, with Meta funding two reactors by 2032 [A/B] ([Wikipedia: TerraPower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TerraPower)); and Fervo's geothermal Cape Station starts in 2026 with up to 500 MW [B] ([Wikipedia: Fervo Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fervo_Energy)). NVIDIA claimed its Blackwell chips cut inference cost and energy "up to 25x" against the previous generation [B/D, vendor claim] ([NVIDIA, 18 Mar 2024](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-blackwell-platform-arrives-to-power-a-new-era-of-computing)). Inference (note 06): fusion is unlikely to undercut orbit before 2035; the real threats are chip and model efficiency, cheap solar plus gas, and relief on grid connection and permitting, which would weaken orbit's scarcity pitch.

#### Lunar programme risk

The lunar landing record, dust, night survival and site scarcity are covered at the end of 2.4: about 38% full success across all landers since 2019 and one clean commercial success in seven missions, with Artemis slipping roughly a year for every year of programme time (inference, note 06).

### 2.8 Financing and valuation: equity on a story, debt only against contracts

#### The capital cycle: a boom built on SpaceX, with a SPAC bust as the counter-precedent

Space venture investment roughly doubled in a year: Seraphim counts **$23 billion in the twelve months to June 2026 against $9.7 billion a year earlier** [A] ([Seraphim Space Index Q2 2026](https://seraphim.vc/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Seraphim-Space-Index-Q2-2026.pdf)), after $12.4 billion in 2025 and $8.4 billion in 2024 ([Seraphim Q4 2025](https://seraphim.vc/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Seraphim-Index-Q4-2025.pdf)). Trackers disagree by a large factor: Space Capital reports a record $36 billion in Q1 2026 alone, against Seraphim's $8.0 billion, because it counts a broader universe; the two series should not be mixed [A] ([Space Capital, 14 Apr 2026](https://www.spacecapital.com/reports/space-investment-quarterly-q1-2026)). Space Capital says orbital data centres have "graduat[ed] from concept to capitalized competition", and Seraphim files Cowboy Space's $275 million round under "Energy & Compute" (same sources).

SpaceX reset the sector's valuations. Its private tender offers valued it at $350 billion (December 2024), $400 billion (July 2025) and $800 billion (December 2025) [A, secondary] ([ValueAdd VC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/spacex-valuation-2026-from-350b-tender-offer-to-2-trillion-market-cap)); the xAI combination in February 2026 valued SpaceX at $1 trillion and xAI at $250 billion [A] ([Bloomberg Law, 3 Feb 2026](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/elon-musks-spacex-said-to-combine-with-xai-ahead-of-mega-ipo)). Its S-1 showed 2025 revenue of $18.7 billion (Starlink $11.4 billion, launch $4.0 billion, xAI $3.2 billion), an operating loss of $2.6 billion, a net loss of $4.9 billion and adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a rough measure of cash profit) of $6.6 billion [A] ([Via Satellite, 20 May 2026](https://www.satellitetoday.com/finance/2026/05/20/spacexs-ipo-filing-gives-first-look-into-companys-financials/)); the xAI segment alone lost $6.355 billion at the operating level in 2025 [A] ([Yahoo Finance on the S-1](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/article/spacex-files-ipo-prospectus-offering-a-peek-into-its-finances-205406189.html)). The IPO priced on 11 June 2026 at $135, raising $75 billion (about $85.7 billion with the over-allotment) at about $1.78 trillion, and closed its first day at $160.95 [A] ([Benzinga, 12 Jun 2026](https://cdn2.benzinga.com/markets/ipos/26/06/53180108/spacex-stock-closes-up-19-in-record-shattering-ipo-debut); [IPOScoop, 11 Jun 2026](https://www.iposcoop.com/the-ipo-buzz-spacex-spcx-raises-75-billion-in-biggest-ipo-in-history/)). By late July it traded below its IPO price (about $1.52 trillion), then recovered to **$167.12 and a $2.27 trillion market value on 5 October 2026**, with trailing revenue of $23.0 billion, a trailing net loss of $8.9 billion and $100 billion of cash [A] ([ValueAdd VC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/spacex-valuation-2026-from-350b-tender-offer-to-2-trillion-market-cap); [stockanalysis SPCX](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/spcx/statistics/)). Analyst Tim Farrar called the million-satellite filing "quite rushed" and "very early stage", aimed at the IPO raise while xAI "sorely needs as much money as possible" [C] ([Implicator, 31 Jan 2026](https://www.implicator.ai/spacex-asks-to-put-one-million-data-centers-in-orbit-the-math-says-otherwise/)). Blue Origin is reported to be seeking about $10 billion of outside money at a $130 billion valuation, its first, with Coatue leading; closing is unconfirmed [B, reported] ([Spokesman-Review/Reuters, 8 Jul 2026](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2026/jul/08/bezos-blue-origin-seeks-first-outside-funding-at-1/)). Starcloud's $1.1 billion to $2.3 billion re-rating in five months (section 2.1) rode the same wave.

The counter-precedent is the 2021 wave of SPACs (shell companies that list first and merge with a private company later). By the end of 2021 eight of nine space SPACs traded below their merger price [A] ([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/?p=122642)); Virgin Orbit merged at a $3.7 billion valuation, could not raise money after its January 2023 launch failure, filed for bankruptcy in April 2023 and sold its assets for about $36 million, under 1% of that valuation [A] ([Wikipedia: Virgin Orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Orbit)); Astra went from a $2.1 billion enterprise value to a take-private at about $11 million [A] ([Wikipedia: Astra Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astra_Space)). The 2025-2026 recovery has been selective: Firefly listed at $45 in August 2025 and opened at $70 but traded at $22.52 on 5 October 2026, while Rocket Lab, AST SpaceMobile and Planet re-rated sharply [A] ([Renaissance Capital](https://www.renaissancecapital.com/IPO-Center/News/112636/Firefly-Aerospace-prices-upsized-IPO-above-the-upwardly-revised-range-at-$4); [stockanalysis FLY](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/fly/statistics/)); Firefly's IPO valuation is reported as both $6.3 billion and $7.4 billion, probably a difference in share counting. Inference (note 07): a technical failure during a cash-burning phase, without a fresh raise, leads to near-total wipe-out, and even the strongest name can trade below its IPO price within weeks.

#### How constellations have actually been financed

There are four proven routes. Corporate balance sheets carry Starlink (inside SpaceX) and Amazon's Kuiper/Leo, which an Amazon executive said would take "$10 billion-plus... before we're gonna see much cash flow" [A] ([Light Reading](https://www.lightreading.com/broadband/amazon-will-put-10b-in-project-kuiper-before-seeing-much-cash-flow-says-exec)). Export-credit agencies (ECAs, government agencies that guarantee loans to buyers of their country's exports) backed Iridium NEXT with a $1.8 billion Coface facility in 2010, mostly fixed at 4.96%, repaid over seven years starting only after the constellation was substantially complete [A] ([Iridium, 4 Oct 2010](https://investor.iridium.com/10-04-2010-iridium-signs-coface-facility-agreement)). Governments lent Telesat C$2.54 billion for Lightspeed in 2024 over 15 years with interest "paid in kind" (added to the loan rather than paid in cash) during construction [A] ([SDxCentral, 16 Sep 2024](https://www.sdxcentral.com/news/telesat-closes-funding-for-lightspeed-leo-network/)). Public-private concessions fund the EU's €10.6 billion IRIS² network, about 60% from the EU budget [A] ([SDxCentral](https://www.sdxcentral.com/news/eu-launches-106bn-space-internet-program-set-to-launch-in-2029/)). The two purely private first-generation low-orbit systems both went bankrupt: the original Iridium in 1999, and OneWeb in March 2020 despite expecting in 2017 to "sell all of its capacity by launch time"; the UK government and India's Bharti rescued OneWeb with $500 million each for about 42% apiece, and Eutelsat valued it at $3.4 billion two years later [A] ([Wikipedia: OneWeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OneWeb)). Inference (note 07): repayment holidays tied to "substantial completion" are the key structural feature, about 5x debt to EBITDA is where mature operators sit, and OneWeb is a clean "distressed sovereign rescue, then partial recovery" precedent.

#### Anchor customers and offtake: real money for defence and landers, almost none for resources

Government demand is large but lumpy. NASA's CLPS programme has a $2.6 billion ten-year ceiling, with lander task orders of about $47-200 million whose final values grew 35-55% above initial awards on the first two missions (inference from contract changes) [A] ([Wikipedia: CLPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services)); Intuitive Machines holds a lunar relay contract worth up to $4.82 billion [A] ([Wikipedia: Intuitive Machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitive_Machines)); Blue Origin's lunar lander contract is $3.4 billion [A] ([Wikipedia: Blue Origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Origin)). Golden Dome has about $37-38 billion appropriated across FY2025-2026 and space-based interceptor awards made in November 2025; a reported $2 billion SpaceX award for a 600-satellite tracking layer is a press report [A/B] ([Wikipedia: Golden Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Dome_%28missile_defense_system%29)). Sovereign AI money is mostly terrestrial: the EU's €200 billion InvestAI with €20 billion for "AI gigafactories" [B] ([European Commission, Feb 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_467)), Saudi Arabia's Humain [B] ([Wikipedia: Humain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humain)) and the 5 GW Stargate UAE [B] ([Wikipedia: Stargate UAE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stargate_UAE)); no sovereign-wealth anchor for orbital compute was found. Inference (note 07): a 2031-2035 orbital-compute venture is likelier to land a defence anchor than a NASA one.

Priced offtake for space resources barely exists: NASA's 2020 regolith purchases totalled $25,001, the US Energy Department's helium-3 order is 3 litres, and Interlune's commercial deals are volume intentions contingent on lunar production with undisclosed prices [A/B] ([NASA, 3 Dec 2020](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-companies-to-collect-lunar-resources-for-artemis-demonstrations/); [SpaceInsider, 8 May 2025](https://spaceinsider.tech/?p=29808)). No orbital-compute take-or-pay or prepayment contract was found anywhere. On the ground, by contrast, AI infrastructure is financed against hyperscaler take-or-pay contracts: CoreWeave's backlog is $99.4 billion (about 40% Meta, 25% OpenAI), and Nebius has deals of $17.4-19.4 billion with Microsoft and $27 billion with Meta [A] ([Daloopa, 3 Jun 2026](https://daloopa.com/blog/analyst-pov/nebius-vs-coreweave-neoclouds-financial-analysis-daloopa-mcp-sourced)). Inference (note 07): lunar "offtake" today is a non-binding demand signal that lenders would not lend against, so equity and grants must carry lunar ventures until production is demonstrated.

#### Lunar companies: small, loss-making and NASA-dependent

Intuitive Machines is worth $3.25 billion with $490 million of trailing revenue after buying Lanteris for $800 million, Firefly $3.77 billion as the only company with a fully successful CLPS landing, and Japan's ispace about ¥60 billion (roughly $0.4 billion, inference on exchange rate) after two crashes, with a weak Altman Z-score of 0.63 (a bankruptcy-risk indicator) [A] ([stockanalysis LUNR](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/lunr/statistics/); [stockanalysis 9348](https://stockanalysis.com/quote/tyo/9348/statistics/); [Wikipedia: ispace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ispace_%28Japanese_company%29)). Astrobotic, whose Peregrine failed, is being absorbed by Voyager (June 2026, price undisclosed) [A] ([Wikipedia: Astrobotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrobotic_Technology)). Interlune had sold only $0.5 million of a $5 million SAFE (a simple agreement for future equity) by January 2026 [A] ([GeekWire, 29 Jan 2026](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/interlune-funding-moon-mining-safe/)). Inference (note 07): the private market for pure lunar mining is thin while orbital-compute startups raise $170-250 million rounds, and lunar value concentrates in diversified companies.

#### The cost of debt: set by the customer's credit, not the hardware

| Rung (inference, note 07) | Example | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Investment-grade hyperscaler contract, ring-fenced asset | CoreWeave DDTL 4.0, Mar 2026, Meta-backed, $7.5-8.5B [A, single secondary source] ([Global Data Center Hub, 7 Apr 2026](https://www.globaldatacenterhub.com/p/is-coreweaves-85-billion-deal-the)) | SOFR + 2.25% (~5.9% fixed) |

Inference (note 07): an orbital data centre in 2031-2035 would price like the second or third rung even with a hyperscaler contract, because insurance limits of $0.3-0.6 billion per risk and unproven hardware add premia ground GPU debt does not carry; CoreWeave's spreads compressed about 1.75 points in eight months when its customer credit improved, a precedent for refinancing on a contract upgrade.

### 2.9 The ground game: terrestrial data centres stay dominant, and time-to-power becomes the scarce asset

#### Demand: doubling by 2030, a wide fork by 2035

Data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024, about 1.5% of world electricity (US 45%, China 25%, Europe 15%) [A], and the IEA's base case has that "more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030", with a 2035 range of 700-1,700 TWh across its cases (base about 1,200) [C] ([IEA Energy and AI, Apr 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary)). Measured demand ran ahead: "Electricity demand from data centres soared by 17% in 2025", AI-focused data centres grew faster, and the five largest tech firms spent over $400 billion of capital in 2025, "set to increase by a further 75% in 2026" [A]; yet "Power consumption per AI task is declining rapidly, with efficiency improving at a rate unprecedented in energy history" [C] ([IEA, 16 Apr 2026](https://www.iea.org/news/data-centre-electricity-use-surged-in-2025-even-with-tightening-bottlenecks-driving-a-scramble-for-solutions)). Moody's expects capex by the largest US cloud and AI companies of about $785 billion in 2026, closing in on $1 trillion in 2027 [C] ([DCD, 14 May 2026](https://datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/moodys-hyperscaler-capex-forecasts-marked-up-by-85bn-to-close-in-on-1trn-by-2027/)). Utility forecasts are far larger and largely phantom: Texas's grid operator ERCOT forecasts a 2032 peak of about 368 GW against a record of about 86 GW, and industry groups say many requests are speculative [C, inflated] ([Texas Tribune, 17 Jun 2026](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/06/17/texas-ercot-data-center-energy-grid/)). Inference (note 09): the IEA's three cases map cleanly onto game eras (about 700 TWh for a bust or efficiency shock, 1,200 base, 1,700 for persistent scarcity), and utility queues should be modelled as a scarcity signal that inflates prices, not as real demand.

#### Power, not land or chips, is the binding near-term constraint

The largest US grid market, PJM, cleared its 2027/28 capacity auction at the $333.44/MW-day cap, 6,623 MW short of its reliability requirement, the first time the whole region fell short [A] ([Renewable Energy World, Dec 2025](https://www.renewableenergyworld.com/power-grid/pjm-capacity-auction-hits-price-cap-again-as-region-falls-short-of-reliability-target)), and its 2028/29 auction on 14 July 2026 cleared at $325 under a cap set with the 13 states' governors and the federal regulator, 6,831 MW short [A] ([PJM, 14 Jul 2026](https://www.pjm.com/-/media/DotCom/about-pjm/newsroom/2026-releases/20260714-pjm-capacity-auction-procures-138318-mw-of-generation-resources.pdf)); POWER Magazine calls it the third straight auction at the ceiling and estimates the uncapped cost at $29.7 billion against $16.4 billion [A/C] ([POWER Magazine, Jul 2026](https://www.powermag.com/pjms-2028-2029-capacity-auction-falls-short-of-reliability-standard-for-second-straight-year/)); POWER and PJM disagree on whether the cap runs only through 2029/30 (prefer PJM). US generation and storage waiting to connect totalled 2,061 GW at the end of 2025, only 13% of 2000-2020 requests ever reached operation, and the median time from request to operation "exceeds 5 years" [A] ([LBNL, 1 Jul 2026](https://emp.lbl.gov/news/backlog-power-plants-seeking-transmission-grid-connection-eased-somewhat-2025-amidst)); northern Virginia data-centre connections take "nearly seven years" (SemiAnalysis, as summarised) [C] ([iTiger](https://www.itiger.com/news/1162677489)). ERCOT had about 474 GW of large loads seeking connection by July 2026, about 90% data centres [A] ([ERCOT, 29 Jul 2026](https://www.ercot.com/files/docs/2026/07/29/ERCOT-Senate-July-29-Panel-1-Assessing-The-Grid.pdf)). GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog plus reservations reached 116 GW in Q2 2026 against planned output of 20-24 GW a year, rising to 30 GW in 2030 [A/B] ([GE Vernova 2Q26, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001996810/000199681026000147/gevpressrelease2q26.htm)); inference (note 09): turbines ordered in late 2026 arrive around 2030-2031. Small modular reactors "come online around 2030" at the earliest [C] ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary)). Operators are responding by building their own power: 65% are deploying on-site generation "by necessity" and 64% rely on gas [C] ([McKinsey, 31 Jul 2026](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/electric-power-and-natural-gas/our-insights/powering-ai-how-real-is-the-risk-of-overbuilding)), and SemiAnalysis expects on-site generation to supply about half of new capacity by 2028 [C] ([SemiAnalysis, 3 Jun 2026](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/to-boldly-go-the-case-for-space-datacenters)). Regulators are converging on a compact: the federal energy regulator FERC ordered in June 2026 faster connection for loads that accept curtailment and "prevention of cost shifting" to other ratepayers [A] ([Brattle, Jul 2026](https://www.brattle.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/FERC-Rulemakings-on-Large-Loads-and-Co-Location.pdf)). Inference (note 09): by the 2030s a firm, grid-connected megawatt that cannot be cut off becomes a premium asset distinct from a "flexible" one, and lifting PJM's price cap would be a legitimate early-2030s shock.

#### Community opposition: from niche to majority in a year

Opposition has become a majority position. Data Center Watch counted "at least 75 projects worth approximately $130 billion" blocked or delayed in Q1 2026, roughly all of 2025's total, and "at least 45 projects worth $68 billion" in Q2 [A] ([Data Center Watch Q1 2026](https://datacenterwatch.org/q1-2026); [Q2 2026](https://www.datacenterwatch.org/q2-2026)); organised opposition groups rose from 396 to 833 in one quarter, strongest in some Republican-led states [A] ([Fortune, 16 Jun 2026](https://www.fortune.com/2026/06/16/data-center-opposition-construction-delays-blocks-report/)). Polling swung from 43% support and 42% opposition in September 2025 to 71% opposed in May 2026 and 75% in August 2026, with utility bills a driving factor [A] ([Heatmap, May 2026](https://heatmap.news/politics/americans-oppose-data-centers-poll); [NationofChange, 21 Aug 2026](https://nationofchange.org/2026/08/21/three-quarters-of-americans-would-oppose-a-data-center-near-their-home-new-polling-finds)); a headline attributing the 71% to Gallup is probably a misattribution. New York's governor paused environmental permits for data centres of 50 MW and above for up to a year by executive order on 14 July 2026 [A] ([Axios, 14 Jul 2026](https://axios.com/2026/07/14/ny-gov-kathy-hochul-data-center-moratorium-executive-order)), while Maine's legislated moratorium was vetoed on 24 April 2026 [A] ([Rockefeller Institute, 10 Jun 2026](https://rockinst.org/blog/updates-on-the-cloud-more-moratoriums-on-data-centers/)); early reports that New York "passed" a pause and that Maine "passes first moratorium" described bills, not law. After one utility introduced ratepayer-protection tariffs, its pipeline of requests fell 81% [A] ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/electric-power-and-natural-gas/our-insights/powering-ai-how-real-is-the-risk-of-overbuilding)). Inference (note 09): the emerging equilibrium is "permit with conditions" rather than bans, which raises costs and delays more than it blocks, and energised, permitted campuses gain value as a moat.

#### Hardware: racks heading to 600 kW, chips the shared bottleneck

NVIDIA's Vera Rubin racks (2026) draw about 120-130 kW, Rubin Ultra "Kyber" racks (2027) about 600 kW, and Feynman follows in 2028, pushing 800-volt power and full liquid cooling [B] ([Introl, 25 Sep 2025](https://introl.com/blog/nvidia-vera-rubin-gpu-600kw-racks-2027); [Jon Peddie Research, Mar 2026](https://www.jonpeddie.com/news/nvidia-gtc-2026-keynote/)). SemiAnalysis argues chipmaking, not power, is the main near-term constraint for both ground and orbit, with AI taking 60-86% of TSMC's leading N3 capacity in 2026-2027 and relief unlikely before 2032-2034 [C] ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/to-boldly-go-the-case-for-space-datacenters)). Inference (note 09): pre-2026 air-cooled campuses need costly retrofits for frontier AI by about 2028-2030, chips depreciate in 3-5 years while power, land and permits last decades, and efficiency gains have so far shown up as more compute per megawatt rather than fewer megawatts.

#### Orbit as threat or niche: what analysts expect

The consensus as of mid-2026 is that underbuilding is the bigger risk to 2030: McKinsey rates overbuild risk "low" because power assets have other customers [C] ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/electric-power-and-natural-gas/our-insights/powering-ai-how-real-is-the-risk-of-overbuilding)). A credible minority disagrees: PwC's Strategy& warns of 50-60 GW of potentially stranded capacity and over $100 billion at risk by 2030 [C] ([Strategy&, 5 Jan 2026](https://www.strategyand.pwc.com/m1/en/industries/energy-chemical-utility-management/gigawatt-bubble.html)), and IEEFA (2025, before the 2026 capex surge) quotes Vistra's chief executive saying queues "may be overstated anywhere from three to five times" [C] ([IEEFA, 3 Jun 2025](https://ieefa.org/resources/risk-ai-driven-overbuilt-infrastructure-real)). SemiAnalysis sees four layers of ground supply (grid connections, converted crypto-mining sites of about 8 GW by 2028, on-site generation, and industrial expansion at more than $20 million per MW) and says space data centres "do not currently threaten terrestrial infrastructure" and "make sense in the world where AI demand well exceeds all of the four layers" [C] ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/to-boldly-go-the-case-for-space-datacenters)). Forethought calls orbital compute "negligible" before 2030 but says that with 10+ reuses per Starship, space could house "much or even majority of compute buildout throughout the 2030s", with training staying on the ground and orbit taking latency-tolerant inference; "Ground costs tighten with scale; orbital costs relax with scale" [C] ([Forethought, 22 May 2026](https://forethought.org/research/will-we-really-put-data-centers-in-space)). Proponents pitch orbit as the escape from these constraints: Musk cites "power, cooling, and community constraints" and says "Space-based AI is obviously the only way to scale in the long term" [D] ([Converge Digest, Feb 2026](https://convergedigest.com/spacex-acquires-xai-betting-on-space-based-ai-compute/)), and Starcloud cites "land availability, grid capacity, water requirements for cooling, permitting timelines, and environmental impact" [D] ([Data Center Frontier, 26 Dec 2025](https://www.datacenterfrontier.com/site-selection/article/55337494/starcloud-launches-orbital-ai-data-center-with-nvidia-h100-gpu)).

| What orbit escapes (inference, note 09) | What orbit does not escape |
|---|---|
| Grid-connection queues and capacity prices | Chip supply, the shared bottleneck |
| Local permitting, opposition and moratoria | Launch licensing and cadence limits |
| Water use | Bandwidth, which confines it mostly to inference |
| | On-orbit failures with no maintenance |
| | Heavy upfront capital and higher cost of capital |

Inference (note 09, consistent with note 10): the best-supported 2031-2035 baseline is that ground campuses remain the dominant and most valuable compute, with orbit a fast-growing but dearer competitor at the margin (about 30% dearer per SemiAnalysis's aggressive case; 1.2-1.6x in the design model's base case), mainly for inference. "Orbit eats the ground" is a defensible alternative scenario only if Starship full reuse, a lifted launch cap and tight ground constraints all arrive together. Orbit interacts with the ground game less by substitution than by setting a ceiling: if orbital inference at about 1.3 times ground cost is available at scale, it caps what owners of scarce new ground capacity can charge and weakens "build here or lose the AI race" arguments against local opposition. Energised, firm, permitted megawatts in constrained hubs hold value; speculative queue positions and unpowered land are where stranding happens.
