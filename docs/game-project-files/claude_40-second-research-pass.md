## Headline: the five corrections most likely to change the live game

1. **Texas demand-response credits are overstated about 3x (§4.7).** Riot's "power credits" mix two things. Its FY2024 10-K splits 2023's $71.2M into $52.6M of power resale and $18.6M of demand response [A, doc 37]; Riot's own statement split August 2023's $31.7M into about $7M from ERCOT's ancillary-services program and about $24M from selling pre-purchased power back to TXU [A]. Doc 38 divided the whole total by MW to get $100K/MW-yr in a hot year. Demand response alone on Rockdale's 700 MW is about $27K/MW-yr in 2023 [D from A]. The resale half is already modelled separately as spot − PPA, so the live game double-counts it.
2. **The hydro-region crypto tariffs were far harsher, and hit existing load (§4.4).** Grant PUD's Rate 17 took a 2 MW miner's all-in rate from 2.6 to 7.9 ¢/kWh over three years [A], and crypto load fell from about 20 MW to under 1 MW once it fully applied in 2020 [A]. Douglas PUD added 10% every six months for five years [B]. Chelan's crypto rate charges energy at a market-indexed price (12.8 ¢ in 2024) [A]. Doc 38's "+60-100% for new load, +30% for existing at renewal" is too mild, and the first moratorium (Chelan) was December 2014, not 2018.
3. **4CP avoidance is worth about $50K/MW-yr, not "−10% of next year's power cost" (§4.7).** Full 4CP exposure runs to about $50,000 per MW-year [C], and Texas retail providers quote transmission-level savings of $47.5-71.1 per kW-year [B]. At a $30-50/MWh contract that is 15-25% of the energy bill.
4. **Iceland's 2018 "power shortage freeze" did not happen as designed; the 2021 refusal did (§4.5).** In February 2018 HS Orka warned it could not supply every proposed project [B]; no freeze was found. On 7 December 2021 Landsvirkjun began rejecting all new crypto-mining power requests and curtailed interruptible industrial supply [B].
5. **The US residential solar credit ended after 2025, and Quebec priced new crypto load at 15 ¢ CAD from June 2018 (§4.1, §4.4).** The 25D credit is 30% 2006-2019, 26% 2020-21, 30% 2022-2025, and zero for homeowners from 2026 [A/B]; doc 38's schedule runs open-ended. Quebec's regulator authorised a 15 ¢/kWh deterrent rate for new blockchain load, about three times what miners had paid [A/B].

Also high-impact for Act III-IV: PJM's capacity credit for 4-hour batteries is not a flat 0.6. It was 50% for 2026/27 and 58% for 2027/28 [A], and PJM's non-binding outlook falls to the 20s-40s% in the 2030s [A].

## Q1. Hydro regions: Chelan, Douglas and Grant County PUDs; Plattsburgh and Massena; Hydro-Québec

### Washington PUDs

**Sourced facts**
- **Chelan, first wave (2014-2016).** Commissioners approved a moratorium on high-density load in December 2014 after a sharp rise in large-power inquiries [B]. In January 2016 bitcoin hosts paid Chelan's commercial rate of 3.40 ¢/kWh or industrial rate of 2.04 ¢/kWh [B]. The first proposal for a high-density rate was 5.036 ¢/kWh, revised in March 2016 to about 4.57 ¢ plus an up-front capital charge [B]. The final "Schedule 35" high-density rate was adopted in July 2016 (NPR, 19 July 2016) for loads using 250 kWh per square foot per year but under 5 average MW, with a five-year phase-in for operations investing $500K+ before 15 December 2014 [B].
- **Schedule 35 terms.** $5.50/kW demand charge plus 2.70 ¢/kWh energy plus an up-front capital charge per kW [A, Chelan tariff]; the 2020 five-year plan steps energy from 2.70 to 3.13 ¢ and demand from $5.50 to $6.40 [A].
- **Chelan, second wave (2018-2019).** Moratorium on new cryptocurrency service applications adopted 19 March 2018 [A]. At that point Chelan had 22 approved high-density customers totalling about 13.5 MW, 19 pending applications for 16.3 MW, and 28 identified unauthorised operations [B]. Requests before the moratorium could have more than doubled the District's 180-200 MW retail load [B]. A crypto rate (Schedule 36) was adopted in December 2018, effective 1 April 2019 [A]. Schedule 36 now charges a market energy rate, $0.1281/kWh from June 2024 [A]. By January 2022 Chelan had under 9 MW of crypto mining operating [A, congressional testimony].
- **Backlash.** Unauthorised miners and denied applicants turned hostile enough that Chelan added bulletproof panels and cameras at headquarters (May 2018) [B]; Chelan was finding two or three unauthorised operations a day [B].
- **Grant PUD.** Since summer 2017 Grant received inquiries for more than 2,000 MW, about 75% from crypto miners, against total generating capacity over 2,100 MW [B]. Rate 17 ("evolving industries") was approved 28 August 2018 with a three-year phase-in from 1 April 2019 [A]. For a 2 MW, 92.5% load-factor customer the all-in rate rises from 2.6 to 7.9 ¢/kWh; for a residential-class miner from 4.9 to 13.7 ¢ [A/B]. Traditional industrial applicants sit in a separate queue that must clear first [A]. When Rate 17 fully applied in 2020, crypto demand fell from about 20 MW to under 1 MW and most new crypto requests were cancelled [A]. In 2026 staff recommended moving crypto out of Rate 17 into a "High Density Compute" class with data centres [B].
- **Douglas PUD.** Moratorium on new crypto and data-centre contracts from 13 May 2019 [B]; lifted January 2020 with crypto rates rising 10% every six months for five years from July 2020 (data centres 5%) [B]. Crypto was 21% of Douglas's customer usage [B].

**Inference [D].** Chelan's Schedule 35 all-in at a 94% load factor: $5.50/kW ÷ (730 h × 0.94) ≈ 0.80 ¢ + 2.70 ¢ ≈ **3.5 ¢/kWh** plus the capital charge. Douglas's +10% every six months for ten steps compounds to 1.1¹⁰ ≈ **2.6x** by 2025.

**Sourced facts**
- Plattsburgh residents paid about 4.5 ¢/kWh and industrial customers about 2 ¢, from St. Lawrence hydropower [B]. The city exceeded its hydropower allotment in December 2017 and January 2018 and bought market power, raising residents' bills [B]. On 15 March 2018 it adopted an 18-month moratorium on new commercial crypto mining, with fines up to $1,000 a day [B].
- In March 2018 the NY PSC allowed Plattsburgh and dozens of other municipal utilities to charge higher rates to crypto miners [B]. ### Hydro-Québec

**Sourced facts**
- Hydro-Québec courted miners in 2017-early 2018, then on 7 June 2018 stopped processing new requests on government order [B]. On 18 June 2018 the Régie authorised a 15 ¢/kWh deterrent rate for new blockchain accounts, about three times what miners had paid; existing clients of about 120 MW were exempt [A/B]. Existing and confirmed load stayed on Rate M or LG [A].

The block requires curtailment on request up to 300 hours a year, and was allocated by RFP scored 30% jobs per MW, 30% payroll per MW, 30% investment per MW, 10% heat recovery [A].
- In November 2022 Hydro-Québec asked the Régie to stop allocating the roughly 270 MW still slated for crypto, citing 14% demand growth to 2032 [B]. In December 2023 it suspended a selected Matane project; the Régie later ruled it must honour its commitment [B].
- Gap: the LG rate in ¢/kWh by year was not obtained. Doc 38's 4-5 ¢ stays a proxy.

## Q5. Solar+storage worked examples (not delivered in doc 37)

All [D], built from sourced inputs. Method: daily load ÷ daily solar yield per kW gives PV size; roughly two-thirds of the energy passes through the battery overnight (≈16 h without useful sun), so add RTE losses; storage sized to the overnight gap, with a 1.5x "bad-week" margin shown separately. CRF 7%: 25-yr PV 0.0858, 10-yr home battery 0.142, 15-yr utility battery 0.1098.

**Example A: 2 kW home rig, 24/7, 2015, sunny US state (18% CF).**
- Load 48 kWh/day. PV yields 4.3 kWh/kW-day → 11.2 kW, +7% for battery losses → **~12 kW** (oversize ratio 6x the load's kW).
- Storage for ~16 h × 2 kW = **~32 kWh usable** (~5 Powerwall-1-class units).
- Capex: 12 kW × $4,300/kW ≈ $52K [2014 median, A]; 32 kWh × $1,020 ≈ $33K [B derived]. Total **≈ $85K before credit**.
- Annual cost: $52K × 0.0858 + $33K × 0.142 + ~$0.3K O&M ≈ $9.5K for 17.5 MWh → **≈ $540/MWh**; with the 30% credit ≈ $380/MWh.
- Bad-week margin (×1.5 PV and storage): ≈ $800/MWh.
- Covers: ~100% on a sunny day; a two-day overcast spell empties it.

**Example B: 1 MW site, 24/7, 2022, Texas (24% CF).**
- Load 24 MWh/day. PV 5.76 MWh/MW-day → 4.2 MW-AC, +10% → **~4.6 MW-AC**.
- Storage **~16 MWh** (four 4-hour MW-class units).
- Capex: 4.6 MW × $1.8M/MW ≈ $8.3M [D, between LBNL's $1.61/W-AC in 2024 and higher 2022 prices]; 16 MWh × $450/kWh ≈ $7.2M [D proxy; no 2022 US turnkey series]. Total **≈ $15.5M**.
- Annual cost: $8.3M × 0.0858 + $7.2M × 0.1098 + ~$0.12M O&M ≈ $1.6M for 8,760 MWh → **≈ $185/MWh**; bad-week margin ≈ $240/MWh.
- Against 2022 ERCOT wholesale prices in the market file, this is several times the grid price.

## Q7. Pumped hydro: global share, closed-loop and former-mine projects, ownership

**Sourced facts**
- Global pumped storage reached 201 GW at end-2025 after a record 11.6 GW added in 2025; 243 GW is under construction and 621 GW in the pipeline [B, IHA via Water Power]. In 2025 global battery capacity (about 257 GW) passed pumped storage (about 202 GW) on a GW basis, though pumped storage still dominates GWh [C]. IHA had earlier put pumped storage at over 94% of installed storage [C]. US pumped storage is about 22-23 GW and over 550 GWh [B].
- **Goldendale (Washington):** 1.2 GW, 12-hour, closed-loop, on the site of a former aluminium smelter. Preliminary permit March 2018, final licence application June 2020, 40-year FERC licence January 2026; construction from 2027 for 4-5 years, commercial operation expected 2031-32; about US$2bn [B]. Owned since November 2020 by Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners (a fund) and developed by Rye Development [B].
- **Lewis Ridge (Kentucky):** 266 MW, 8-hour, on former mine lands; final licence application July 2025 [B].
- More than 60 GW of pumped storage is in development across the US [B]. Round-trip efficiency is around 80% [C].

**Inference [D].** Goldendale pitch: $2bn ÷ 1.2 GW ≈ **$1,670/kW**, i.e. $139/kWh at 12 h, against NREL ATB's $3,755-4,234/kW class average (doc 37 [A]). Permit-to-operation ≈ 13-14 years, of which licensing ≈ 8 years.

## Q10. Part D: the world 2036-2040 (for Act V)

### D2. AI compute and chips to 2040

- Training compute for frontier AI has grown about 4x per year; Epoch finds runs of 2e29 FLOP likely feasible by 2030, and estimates a frontier training run in 2030 would need roughly 6 GW at current efficiency trends (range 1-45 GW by power-sourcing strategy) [C, Epoch AI 2024].
- imec's 2026 roadmap: A14-class in 2028; A10 (1 nm-class) around 2030-31, after which contacted poly pitch stops scaling; CFET likely from A7 around 2033; 0.3 nm-class (A3) around 2038 [C, via Tom's Hardware/TrendForce]. Density gains after 2030 come from cell-height reduction, CFET stacking and advanced packaging rather than gate pitch [C].
- **Inference [D]:** per-chip efficiency gains slow from the mid-2030s, so total AI power demand tracks compute demand more closely in Act V than in Acts III-IV. The roadmap's published horizon ends around 2038-39; anything after is unsourced.

### D3. Orbit after 2035

- Doc 31's SemiAnalysis base case (parity ~2040) stands. Its conditional: orbital levelised costs start about 4x terrestrial in 2026 and only fall below terrestrial around 2040 [C].
- A Google research paper estimates launch prices could fall below $200/kg around 2035 if a ~20% learning rate holds, which needs about 180 Starship launches a year [C]. Forethought's analysis puts orbital data centres cost-competitive with a bullish terrestrial buildout at about $100/kg [C]. Current Falcon 9 cost is about $600/kg (Bain) [C]; Goldman Sachs's bullish case has Starship at $183/kg in 2030 [C].
- In early 2026 SpaceX filed with the FCC for up to one million data-centre satellites [C, as reported by Forethought].

### D4. The Moon after 2035

- Artemis II flew the first crewed lunar flyby in April 2026 [A, CRS]. Artemis III (mid-2027) will demonstrate landers in Earth orbit; the crewed landing moves to 2028 (Artemis IV) [A/B]. NASA paused Gateway and announced a lunar base in March 2026; phase 2 (2029-early 2030s) builds permanent infrastructure including a power grid; extended-stay habitats come in phase 3 during the 2030s [B]. NASA plans to test water extraction and fuel production before long-term presence [B].
- The Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel said lander complexity and delays cast doubt on the landing timeline [A, via CRS].
- **Lunar production after 2035: no sourced schedule found.** Any production plant date is [D].

### D5. Bitcoin in the late 2030s

- Halving schedule (protocol, [A]): every 210,000 blocks. Next halvings ≈ 2028 (1.5625 BTC), 2032 (0.78125), 2036 (0.390625), 2040 (0.1953125). Dates are approximate (block-time dependent).
- Fees have been about 1-2% of miner revenue in 2025-26, with short spikes (e.g. 37.6 BTC of fees in block 840,000 at the April 2024 halving) [C]. Analysts' security-budget models need fees to rise roughly 55x for 2028 and 80x for 2032 to hold miner revenue at today's levels without price appreciation [C]; one estimate puts the security budget below 0.15% of market cap by 2036 [C]. Tail emission is discussed as a possible protocol change [C].
- **Inference [D]:** the market file's hashprice should fall in steps at each halving unless price or fees compensate; a "fee market matures" outcome and a "security scare" outcome are both defensible for 2036-40.

### D6. Quantum and Q-Day

- NIST's draft IR 8547 (Nov 2024) deprecates RSA, ECDSA and Diffie-Hellman after 2030 and disallows them after 2035; OMB has adopted 2035 as the federal full-migration date [A/B].
- Gidney (May 2025) estimated RSA-2048 could be factored with fewer than one million physical qubits, down from about 20 million in 2019 [A, preprint; via GRI]. The Global Risk Institute's 2025 survey of 26 experts puts the chance of a cryptographically relevant quantum computer within 10 years at 28-49%, and likely (51-70%) within 15 years [C].
- **Inference [D]:** a CRQC event inside 2036-2040 is plausible (roughly 35-60% by 2040 on the survey's numbers). Bitcoin uses ECDSA/Schnorr signatures; a post-quantum signature migration would be a visible protocol event before or after a CRQC.

### D7. Grid and policy to 2040

- DOE directed FERC (Oct 2025) to set large-load interconnection rules for loads over 20 MW, with expedited (60-day) studies for curtailable loads and 100% of network upgrades charged to the large load [B]. FERC approved SPP's High Impact Large Load process (Jan 2026), ordered PJM co-location rules (Dec 2025), committed to act by June 2026, and in June 2026 issued show-cause orders to all six RTOs/ISOs [A/B]. PJM's mechanism is unlikely to be fully active before 2027 [B].
- The residential 25D credit ended after 2025; third-party-owned systems can still use 48E [A/B].

## Q11. Small wind: realised capacity factors and why

**Sourced facts**
- Warwick Wind Trials (UK, 26 building-mounted turbines, one year): average capacity factor 0.85%, or 4.15% after excluding downtime and imported energy, against 10-30% for larger free-standing turbines [A/C, trial report].
- Energy Saving Trust field trial (57 turbines, 2009): free-standing turbines averaged 19%; building-mounted about 3%; the recommended wind-speed estimation method overestimated urban and suburban sites [B].
- Massachusetts (21 small turbines, 2008): systems produced about 29% of the energy installers predicted in grant applications; no installer or model met its target [A, MassCEC].
- US validation study: SAM, Wind Report, MyWindTurbine.com and Global Wind Atlas overestimated actual annual capacity factors by 2.5, 4.2, 11.5 and 7.3 percentage points [A, PNNL].
- Reasons given across studies: overestimated site wind speed, turbulence at low hub heights, optimistic power curves, and downtime [A/B/C].
